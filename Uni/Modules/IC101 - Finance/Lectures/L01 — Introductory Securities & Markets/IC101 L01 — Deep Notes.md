## 1. What is Finance? Why do we need it?
**Exam-ready definition:** *Finance is the process of allocating scarce capital across people and time, while assessing, pricing and transferring risk.*

В самом начале книги автор выделяет **risk and price**. При выдаче кредита банк оценивает вероятность невозврата и закладывает её в **interest rate**. При выпуске shares компания продаёт инвесторам долю в будущих результатах бизнеса — вместе с риском.

### 1.1 Human economic lifecycle (pp. 3–4)
- **Young adults:** часто есть расходы на education, housing, consumption, но ещё мало income. Финансирование может поступать от семьи либо через borrowing.
- **Working life:** income может вырасти; когда income > consumption, появляются **savings**.
- **Unemployment / illness:** income может снизиться → помогают **savings** или borrowing.
- **Retirement:** employment income обычно падает, и человек использует **pension pot**, другие savings и pension income.

> [!example] Borrow today vs save today
> **Borrow today → spend now → repay from future income.**  
> **Save today → invest now → spend in retirement.**  
> Это **intertemporal transfer of spending power**; в масштабах общества оно нередко также **intergenerational**.

**Nuance:** Не каждый молодой человек берёт кредит, и не каждый человек среднего возраста каждый месяц сберегает. Это типичная модель, а не жизненное правило.

### 1.2 Participants & movement of money
| Participant | Role | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Savers / Lenders** | Have funds not needed for immediate spending | Households, pension funds |
| **Borrowers** | Receive funding with a promise to repay | Households, firms, governments |
| **Issuers** | Issue securities to raise funds | Company issues shares; government issues bonds |
| **Financial Intermediaries** | Connect funding needs with savings and risk capacity | Commercial banks, investment banks |
| **Buy Side** | Invest capital into assets | Pension funds, asset managers |
| **Sell Side** | Provide distribution, trading, underwriting and related services | Investment banks / broker-dealers |

**Core flow:** Savings → financial system → financing → real projects. **Cash flows and risk** then flow through the structure of financial claims.  
**Important:** buying an existing share in the **secondary market** usually pays the previous shareholder, **not** fresh cash directly to the company; new funding mainly arises at **primary issuance**.

### 1.3 Financial vs Real Investment (pp. 12–13)
- **Financial Investment:** investor buys a *financial claim* — shares or bonds. For a new issue, funds go to an issuer.
- **Real Investment:** firm uses funds for factories, machinery, technology, research or workers.

> [!example] New product
> Investors buy **newly issued shares** of a start-up → the start-up buys equipment and hires engineers → potential future sales create cash flow → investor expects dividends or capital gain, but bears **Equity Risk**.

### 1.4 Why Finance matters for economic growth (pp. 6–8)
Ресурсы ограничены (**scarce resources**). Компании не могут финансировать все проекты сразу. Capital markets и credit markets помогают выбирать, кто получит финансирование и по какой цене.

- **Capital Widening:** capital grows to support *more workers*. Example: buy 20 extra machines and employ 20 workers.
- **Capital Deepening:** more capital *per worker*. Example: upgrade each worker’s machinery, potentially raising productivity.
- **Venture Capital:** finances uncertain innovative projects.

> [!important] Don't misread the author
> Автор **не** говорит, что capital важнее technology или natural resources. Он говорит, что без financing полезные знания и ресурсы часто невозможно превратить в productive investment.

### 1.5 Market vs Non-market allocation (pp. 7, 10–11)
**Price mechanism:** shortage → price rises → potential higher profit attracts producers/resources → supply may increase. Price signals scarcity and opportunity.  
**Central planning:** central authorities allocate production, investment and goods administratively. Author uses China’s move toward decentralised decisions and more market prices as illustration.  
**Limitation:** цены могут давать и плохие сигналы; **market failure** и financial bubbles способны направить capital не туда.

### 1.6 Public interest and the financial sector (pp. 4–5)
Автор противопоставляет **financing the real economy** и чрезмерный **proprietary trading** банков за свой счёт. Это не значит, что bank profit плох: прибыль от хорошего lending может сочетаться с реальной пользой. Вопрос — насколько деятельность банка способствует productive allocation и каким risk подвергает общество. В книге упоминаются исторические предложения налогов на trading, а не доказательство, что они уже введены.

## 2. Price vs Value & The Three Pillars
### 2.1 Market Price vs Fundamental Value (pp. 9–10, 21–24)
- **Market Price:** what buyers and sellers actually pay today.
- **Fundamental Value / Intrinsic Value:** estimate built from future **cash flows**, their **timing** and **risk**.
- **Mispricing:** market price diverges materially from a sound fundamental estimate. **Bubble:** sustained price far above defensible fundamentals.

> [!example] £100 share
> Market Price = £100. Your well-supported DCF estimate = £80. The share **may** be overvalued. Но оценка £80 зависит от прогнозов и discount rate; не является гарантированной «истинной ценой».

### 2.2 Three Pillars — *not* three separate asset classes (pp. 13–20)
| Pillar | Question answered | Main tools |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Time Value of Money** | How to compare cash at different dates? | PV, FV, compounding, discounting, NPV |
| **2 — Risk Management** | How uncertain is return and how can risk be handled? | Diversification, Insurance, Hedging |
| **3 — Asset Pricing** | How much should an asset be worth for its cash flows and risk? | DCF, comparable multiples, bond yields |

**Critical correction to the old notes:** **Diversification is a technique within Pillar 2, not Pillar 3.** The third pillar is **Asset Pricing**.

## 3. Pillar 1 — Time Value of Money (TVM)
**Definition:** *Money available now is generally worth more than the same nominal sum later, because it can earn a return and timing affects choice.*

### 3.1 Formulas
$$FV=PV(1+r)^t$$
$$PV=\frac{FV}{(1+r)^t}$$

- **PV (Present Value)** — today’s equivalent of a future amount.
- **FV (Future Value)** — amount at a future date.
- **r** — rate per period as decimal (5% = 0.05).
- **t** — number of periods, consistent with r.
- **Compounding** moves forward in time; **Discounting** moves backward.

> [!example] Slide example
> Receive **£1,100 in one year**. Discount Rate **5%**.  
> $$PV=\frac{1100}{1.05}=\pounds1,047.62$$
> Значит, при 5% эквивалент будущих £1,100 сегодня — примерно £1,047.62.

> [!example] Compound Interest
> Invest £100 for 2 years at 5% annually:  
> $$FV=100(1.05)^2=\pounds110.25$$
> Второй год проценты начисляются также на проценты первого года.

### 3.2 Opportunity Cost: MSc example (pp. 14–15)
Автор сравнивает решение учиться дальше с вариантом сразу работать:

| Cost | Amount |
|---|---:|
| MSc course fee | £18,000 |
| Extra accommodation and food | £6,000 |
| Foregone salary (**Opportunity Cost**) | £21,000 |
| **Total economic cost before financing effects** | **£45,000** |

**Decision:** насколько MSc поднимет future earnings? Особенно важно, **когда** придёт дополнительный доход — сразу после учёбы или лишь спустя десятилетия. Если деньги заимствованы, учти borrowing cost; даже собственные деньги имеют alternative investment return.  
**Note:** £21,000 — *not cash paid to university*, а зарплата, от которой студент отказался.

### 3.3 Corporate decision-making & NPV (pp. 15–17)
Компании сопоставляют **initial outflows** и ожидаемые **future inflows**:

$$NPV=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}-I_0$$

Здесь $I_0$ = initial investment, $CF_t$ = net future cash flow, $r$ = appropriate project discount rate.

- **NPV > 0** → проект предполагаемо увеличивает shareholder value при выбранных прогнозах.
- **NPV < 0** → при тех же прогнозах проект не покрывает требуемую доходность.
- **Cost of Capital** отражает цену привлечённого capital и его risk; компании часто рассматривают **WACC**, но ставка проекта должна соответствовать *риску этого проекта*.

> [!example] £1,000 project
> Invest £1,000 now. Receive £1,100 next year. Discount at 5%:  
> $$NPV=\frac{1100}{1.05}-1000\approx\pounds47.62$$
> Положительный NPV в этой упрощённой модели.

**Textbook case (historical):** Nokia и LG пытались конкурировать в смартфонах; автор описывает реакцию инвесторов LG на fundraising plans как сомнение в способности management вложить ресурсы с достаточной отдачей. **Суть:** cash raised ≠ good investment; важен NPV.

### 3.4 Discount Rate & Risk Premium
Упрощённое объяснение:

$$\text{Required Return} \approx \text{Risk-free Rate}+\text{Risk Premium}$$

Выше требуемая доходность $r$ → ниже PV для тех же положительных cash flows. Исторически **government bond yields** использовались как ориентир risk-free; в реальном мире у них тоже может быть credit risk и другие риски.

## 4. Pillar 2 — Risk, Return & Risk Management
### 4.1 Basic concepts (pp. 17–18)
- **Expected Return:** expected outcome of investment, not a promise.
- **Realised Return:** outcome actually achieved.
- **Risk:** uncertainty about outcomes; in common financial models, measured by return dispersion/variance.
- **Risk Aversion:** an investor prefers less risk for the same expected return.
- **Risk Premium:** extra expected return required for bearing risk.

> [!warning] Exam trap
> **Higher risk may justify higher *expected* return; it never guarantees higher *realised* return.** A risky share can lose most of its value.

**Holding Period Return** for an investment with cash income:
$$HPR=\frac{P_1-P_0+D}{P_0}$$
$D$ = dividends/income paid during holding period (if any).

### 4.2 Three management methods
| Tool | What it does | Student example |
|---|---|---|
| **Diversification** | Reduces dependence on one asset/company | £1,000 split across sectors, not one share |
| **Hedging** | Offsets specified exposure | UK business fixes exchange rate for USD receipt |
| **Insurance** | Transfers specified risk for premium | Business insures property against fire |

**Risk types:** Credit Risk, Equity Risk, Currency Risk, Interest Rate Risk, Commodity Price Risk.  
**Risk transfer:** shares move part of business risk to shareholders; loans put credit exposure on lenders; derivatives can reallocate price risk.

## 5. Pillar 3 — Asset Pricing
### 5.1 DCF: cash flows, time, risk (pp. 23–24)
**DCF (Discounted Cash Flow):** value is built from present values of expected future cash flows.
$$V_0=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{E(CF_t)}{(1+r)^t}$$

Actual company valuation usually needs suitable cash flow definition, explicit forecast period and terminal assumptions, and an appropriate Discount Rate. A valuation is an **estimate**, not a guaranteed sale price.

### 5.2 Bonds, coupons, yields (pp. 18–19, 23–25)
- **Bond:** debt claim issued by a government or company.
- **Principal / Face Value:** contractual repayment amount at maturity (for a plain-vanilla bond).
- **Coupon Rate:** contractual rate used to compute coupon on face value.
- **Market Yield / Yield to Maturity:** return implied by current **price** and promised cash flows (assuming contractual payments are made).
- **Credit Risk:** issuer may not pay; corporate bonds typically require a **Credit Spread** over otherwise comparable lower-risk bonds.

> [!example] Textbook bond (rounded)
> £/$1,000 **Face Value**, **5% Coupon Rate** → $50 coupon every year. When 8 years remain, comparable bonds yield **7%**. The existing bond must trade **below par** (about $881) to be competitive. Why? Investor still collects $50 per year, plus $1,000 at maturity, but pays less today.

> [!important] Bond rule
> **Market yields ↑ → fixed-coupon bond prices ↓**; **market yields ↓ → prices ↑**, other factors equal.  
> **Coupon Rate ≠ Current Yield ≠ Yield to Maturity.** Coupon stays fixed; market price changes.

### 5.3 Comparables: P/E and P/B (pp. 21–23, 26)
**P/E (Price/Earnings):**
$$P/E=\frac{\text{Share Price}}{\text{Earnings Per Share}}$$

> [!example] P/E
> Share Price £100; **EPS** £10 → **P/E = 10**. Investors pay £10 per £1 of annual earnings per share. The multiple may be higher because of expected growth; or because the market is too optimistic. Compare companies with similar risk and prospects.

**P/B (Price/Book):**
$$P/B=\frac{\text{Market Capitalisation}}{\text{Book Value of Equity}}$$

> [!example] P/B
> Bank market cap £5bn, reported **Book Value of Equity** £10bn → **P/B = 0.5**. This can reflect low expected profitability, doubts about asset values or other concerns; **not** automatic proof of asset misstatement.

**Franchise Value:** extra value from an enterprise's ability to earn attractive returns, brand, skills and relationships, beyond what its stand-alone assets may justify.

### 5.4 Law of One Price & arbitrage (pp. 21–22)
**Law of One Price:** identical cash flows, maturity and risk should command the same price in competitive markets without meaningful frictions.

> [!example] Arbitrage
> Same asset trades for £100 in one venue and £105 elsewhere (assuming same currency, immediate transfer and no fees). Buy at £100, sell at £105 → £5 **gross** arbitrage gain. Trading tends to close the gap.  
> На практике комиссии, funding costs, liquidity, restrictions и timing не дают гарантии идеального выравнивания.

**Valuation Models:** since no two assets are perfectly alike, analysts use *similar* assets and adjust for differences (e.g., house price comparables or company P/E multiples).

### 5.5 Bank assets: what does 'value' mean? (pp. 25–26)
- **Amortised Cost:** accounting measurement based on original carrying amount, adjusted for repayments, amortisation and applicable impairment/expected credit loss rules.
- **Mark-to-Market / Fair Value:** measurement reflecting current market prices or modelled market-participant assumptions.
- **Banking Book vs Trading Book:** textbook contrasts longer-held lending positions with assets actively traded; accounting and regulatory treatment is more nuanced than a universal one-to-one rule.

**Why it matters:** in stress, forced-sale prices may diverge from a DCF-based estimate. A bank's accounting carrying value, traded asset price and shareholders' market valuation can all differ.  
**Figure 1.2 (historical):** banking **P/B** multiples fell sharply around the global financial crisis; this reflects changed investor expectations and risk perception, not simply a change in book accounting.

## 6. Asset Types (pp. 18–20)
| Type | What produces value? | Examples | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Capital Assets / Cash-Market Instruments** | Expected cash flows / contractual or ownership claims | Bonds, shares, loans | Credit, business, rates |
| **Derivatives** | Linked to underlying price, rate, index or event | Futures, options, swaps | Market, leverage, counterparty |
| **Real Assets** | Productive/physical use and/or sale value | Real estate, oil, wheat | Price, demand, liquidity |
| **Speculative Stores of Value** | Mainly resale demand/subjective value, often no periodic cash flow | Gold, art, precious stones | Sentiment, price, liquidity |

### 6.1 Bond vs Share
| | **Bond** | **Share** |
|---|---|---|
| Economic claim | Lender / creditor | Ownership / shareholder |
| Typical payoff | Coupons + repayment of principal | Possible dividends + resale value |
| Fixed maturity | Usually yes | Usually no |
| Risks | Default and yield moves | Business performance and market price |

**Important nuance:** derivatives can have intermediate cash flows (e.g., swaps), and real estate can earn rents; the categories are explanations from the chapter, not rigid universal rules.

### 6.2 Sources of cash flow (pp. 18–19)
- **Employment income** → supports household debt repayments.
- **Business cash flow** → supports corporate debt, potential shareholder payments.
- **Tax revenue** → services sovereign debt.
- **Rental income** → helps service property financing and reward owners.

## 7. EMH, Market Efficiency & Market Failure (pp. 8–10, 21–22)
### 7.1 Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)
**EMH:** market prices reflect relevant information rapidly (under the specified form of efficiency). A standard implication is that investors cannot consistently earn abnormal **risk-adjusted** returns merely by trading on information already priced in.

| Form | What prices are assumed to reflect? |
|---|---|
| **Weak Form** | Past market-price and trading data |
| **Semi-strong Form** | All publicly available information |
| **Strong Form** | Public and private information |

> [!note] Coverage
> The chapter introduces EMH conceptually. The three-form classification is included in your existing lecture notes as a **study extension**, not presented as a detailed three-form framework in this excerpt of the textbook.

### 7.2 Two separate meanings of 'efficient'
**Informational Efficiency:** prices react to new information.  
**Allocative Efficiency:** capital is allocated to the most productive uses, improving economic output/welfare under appropriate conditions.

**Important:** fast price adjustment does **not** automatically prove assets are fundamentally well valued. Models, risk beliefs and incentives can be wrong.

### 7.3 Market Failure & bubbles
- **Market Failure:** markets fail to allocate resources efficiently.
- **Asset Bubble:** quoted prices rise beyond defensible fundamental valuation.
- The textbook points to US housing/mortgage assets and some eurozone sovereign debt **before and during the 2007–10 crises** as historical cases of risk/mispricing concern.

> [!example] Why mispricing matters
> If lenders underprice **Credit Risk** on mortgages, mortgages become too easy/cheap to obtain; this may encourage overbuilding and inflate house prices. Later losses reveal that productive resources were allocated poorly.

### 7.4 How do Arbitrage and EMH differ?
- **Arbitrage:** *trading mechanism* that can shrink price gaps for equivalent payoffs.
- **Law of One Price:** *valuation principle* for identical assets.
- **EMH:** *hypothesis* about prices reflecting information.
- Neither proves that *every* market quote always equals fundamental value.

## 8. Extra textbook illustrations (read for understanding, not memorisation)
**Ancient Egypt (p. 5):** save grain during years of abundance so the country can use stocks in years of poor harvest. Intuition behind **countercyclical policy** — build buffers in good times. If savings are insufficient, a country may need external borrowing; over-borrowing can create debt crises.

**China & central planning (pp. 10–11):** contrast central administrative allocation with decisions based on price signals and decentralisation; the text dates from 2012 and describes reform history, not today's detailed policy.

**2007–08 crisis:** a reminder that optimism and low apparent risk can cause **mispricing** and inefficient capital allocation.

## 9. Formulas — one place
| Formula | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $FV=PV(1+r)^t$ | Compounding |
| $PV=FV/(1+r)^t$ | Discounting |
| $NPV=\sum CF_t/(1+r)^t-I_0$ | Project value added |
| $V_0=\sum E(CF_t)/(1+r)^t$ | Simple DCF model |
| $HPR=(P_1-P_0+D)/P_0$ | Holding Period Return |
| $P/E=\text{Share Price}/EPS$ | Earnings multiple |
| $P/B=\text{Market Cap}/\text{Book Equity}$ | Book multiple |

> [!warning] Formula hygiene
> Не путай **Coupon Rate** с **Yield**; **Expected Return** с **Realised Return**; **Discount Rate** с central-bank policy rate. Формулы выше — учебные упрощения и требуют соответствующих предположений.
