# IC101 L02 — Explanation

> [!info] Пошаговое объяснение (сгенерировано). Лекция: [[IC101 L02 — Balance sheets, funding & financial intermediaries]]. Ссылки *(Slide N; MM:SS)* ведут к источнику — сверяйся с ними.

## Overview

This lecture moves from last week's 'how to value things' toolkit to the 'machine' that actually moves money from savers to companies (Slide 2). The central tool is the balance sheet: assets = liabilities + equity, and the key idea that every asset is somebody else's liability (Slides 2, 4, 8). From the right-hand side of the balance sheet the lecture develops the debt-versus-equity choice, leverage, business vs financial risk, Modigliani-Miller and the tax shield, what happens when a firm fails, the intermediaries that carry money to firms, and finally the primary and secondary markets where securities are issued and traded (Slides 3, 7-24). It matters because, as the lecturer says, a bank or a company 'is' a balance sheet: when the balance sheet breaks, the institution dies (Slide 2; 11:54).

## 1. Where we came from: discounting, time and risk

The lecturer starts by linking back to Lecture 1. Last week's message was: a price is future cash, adjusted for **time** and **risk** (Slide 2). To value a security you ask what cash you will receive in the future, and then you *discount* it back into today's value (01:00-05:00).

Why discount? For two reasons given in the recording: (a) money grows by itself, so £100 today is worth more than £100 later, and inflation means £100 buys less in the future; (b) risk, because future cash is only a promise — the company might go bankrupt or not deliver. Both are put in the denominator of the valuation formula, and in finance we call that discount rate the **required rate of return**: the minimum return you demand before you invest (about 03:00-05:30).

This week the question changes: not 'what is it worth?' but 'HOW does the money actually move from savers to the companies that use it?' (Slide 2). *(Slide 2; 00:00; 03:00; 05:30)*

**Key points:**

- Price = future cash flows adjusted for time and risk (Slide 2).
- We discount because money grows passively, because of inflation, and because future cash is uncertain.
- The discount rate is usually called the required rate of return; if a security does not offer it, a professional will not invest.
- This week's new question: how money travels from savers to firms — through balance sheets.

**Examples:**

- A bond promises fixed payments, but 'what if that company goes bankrupt?' — so a risk element must be taken out of the future cash flows (04:00).

## 2. What a balance sheet is — starting with your own

A balance sheet has exactly three parts: **assets** (what you own), **liabilities** (what you owe) and **equity** (the owners' stake). The lecturer makes this personal before making it corporate: your assets might be clothes, cash in the bank, maybe a car; your liabilities might be a student loan, a credit card, or money your parents lent you if the agreement is that you repay it (06:09-10:00).

Equity is harder in personal finance. In the class discussion the suggestion was that your parents have 'equity in you' because they have invested in you, while the lecturer pushed back on the idea that the government is an equity holder in a person (around 09:00). The useful part is the logic: if your assets fall so low that you can no longer support what you owe, you are in trouble — you can become bankrupt. The same logic works for companies.

The lecturer calls a balance sheet 'the X-ray of the financial position of any organization' and says every organisation has one. A risk manager he once met said balance sheets should be taught in school (06:09, 11:54). *(06:09; 09:00; 11:54; Slide 4)*

**Key points:**

- Three elements only: assets, liabilities, equity.
- Assets = what you own; liabilities = what you owe; equity = the owners' stake (Slide 4).
- If assets cannot support what you owe, bankruptcy follows — at personal and at company level.
- The balance sheet is the 'X-ray' of any organisation and, for finance, the most important financial statement (11:54).

**Examples:**

- Personal assets: clothes, cash in the bank, a car. Personal liabilities: student loan, credit card, money borrowed from parents (06:09-08:30).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The 'parents have equity in you' idea is a teaching analogy, not an accounting rule. Treat it as intuition only: equity means a residual, non-contractual claim, and that is why it is 'more nuanced' for a person than for a company.

## 3. The one equation: assets = liabilities + equity

The accounting equation is **Assets = Liabilities + Equity** (Slide 4). The left side shows what the firm owns. The right side shows who funded it: lenders (liabilities, what the firm owes) and owners (equity). The two sides must always balance — 'that is the whole idea' (Slide 4). The lecturer puts it as: everything you own has to be financed either by debt holders or by equity holders; there is no third way (11:54).

Rearranged, the equation says Equity = Assets − Liabilities. If that number is positive, the firm could pay everyone off and still have something left: it is **solvent** and has an 'equity cushion'. If it is negative, it owes more than it owns and it can go bankrupt. This is why the lecturer says analysts, lenders and regulators look at those three numbers first (around 13:00-14:30).

The lecturer also warns, through examples, what happens when the balance sheet breaks: Silicon Valley Bank and Northern Rock both 'died' — they went bankrupt (Slide 2; 11:54). *(Slide 2; Slide 4; Slide 26; 11:54; 13:00)*

**Key points:**

- Assets = Liabilities + Equity, always (Slide 4).
- Equity = Assets − Liabilities: positive = solvent with a cushion; negative = can go bankrupt.
- Only two sources of funding exist: lenders and owners.
- Lenders have the prior (first) claim; equity holders are paid after, so they carry more risk (13:00).

**Examples:**

- Exam-style check from Slide 26: assets £100m, liabilities £80m, so equity = £20m.
- Silicon Valley Bank and Northern Rock: when the balance sheet broke, the bank died (Slide 2; 11:54).

## 4. Reading Apple's balance sheet: current vs non-current

The lecture reads a real balance sheet: Apple Inc., FY2025 ($bn). Assets total $359bn, split into **non-current assets $211bn** and **current assets $148bn**. The funding side also totals $359bn: **liabilities $285bn** (current $166bn + non-current $119-120bn) and **equity $74bn** (Slides 4, 5).

The vocabulary rule the lecturer tells you to write down: 'current' means short term (within a year) and 'non-current' means long term. Non-current assets are things held for years — property, plant and equipment, data centres, intellectual property, long-term investments. Current assets can be turned into cash within a year — cash and short-term investments (about $55bn), inventories (unsold phones), and receivables (money customers or partners owe Apple, for example monthly app subscriptions expected over the next year). Current liabilities are bills due within a year, such as suppliers; non-current liabilities are long-term debt, which for Apple is mostly bonds sold to investors (16:00-23:00; Slides 5, 6).

The lecturer adds a nuance: the difference between 'liquid' and 'short-term' is partly your *intention*. A 10-year bond can be sold quickly, but if you intend to hold it for ten years it is a long-term investment (17:57). Equity has two parts: share capital (money investors paid for shares) and retained profits, because profits kept in the business belong to the shareholders (around 22:00).

The punchline: Apple's assets are funded roughly **20% equity, 80% liabilities**. A company worth over $3 trillion carries only about $74bn of equity on its books — 'hold that, it's a clue' (Slide 4; 23:00). Slide 6 gives every line in full; the lecturer said that level of detail is 'not important for this year' but can be useful if you want to go deeper (23:30). *(Slide 4; Slide 5; Slide 6; 16:00; 17:57; 20:30; 23:00)*

**Key points:**

- Current = within a year; non-current = longer than a year. Learn this wording because real balance sheets use it.
- Apple FY2025: total assets $359bn = liabilities $285bn + equity $74bn (Slide 4).
- Current assets include cash, short-term investments, inventories and receivables; current liabilities include amounts owed to suppliers (Slides 5, 6).
- Equity = share capital + retained profits (Slide 6 shows share capital $94bn and retained earnings −$14bn after buybacks).
- Apple is funded about 20% by equity and 80% by liabilities (Slide 4; 23:00).

**Examples:**

- A customer paying a monthly app subscription creates a receivable for Apple, because Apple expects that cash within the year (20:30).
- A 10-year bond: liquid (sellable) but still a long-term asset if you intend to hold it (17:57).

## 5. The system is linked balance sheets

This is the sentence the lecture calls 'the one that unlocks it all': **every asset is somebody else's liability** (Slide 2). Slide 8 traces one pound of your savings. You put £1,000 in the bank: that is *your* asset. For the bank it is a *liability*, because the bank owes you the money. The bank lends the £1,000 to a company: that loan is the *bank's asset* and the *company's liability*. The company uses the money to build and grow (Slide 8; 24:14).

Returns flow back down the chain in the opposite direction: the company makes a profit, the bank earns something, and you receive interest. The lecturer says this is exactly last week's savers-to-borrowers loop, and calls it 'the building block of the whole capitalist system' (Slide 8; 25:00).

So a balance sheet is not just a company document. The whole financial system is balance sheets chained together: yours, the bank's, the company's. *(Slide 2; Slide 8; 24:14; 25:00)*

**Key points:**

- Every asset is someone else's liability (Slide 2, Slide 8).
- Your £1,000 deposit = your asset, the bank's liability.
- The bank's loan = the bank's asset, the company's liability.
- Interest and profit flow back down the chain the other way (Slide 8).

**Examples:**

- Saver → bank → company, with £1,000 followed step by step (Slide 8; 24:14).

## 6. Balance-sheet X-ray: which one is the bank?

Slide 21 shows three real firms with funding as a share of total assets and asks the class to guess which is the bank. Slide 22 reveals: **Tesco (supermarket) ~25% equity**, **Lloyds (bank) ~5% equity**, **Schroders (asset manager) ~65% equity** (figures stylised from 2024 annual reports).

Why does a bank run on so little equity? Because, as the lecturer explains, borrowing *is* the business of banking. A bank takes in deposits and borrowed money and lends it out, and the margin is thin — he says banks work on only about 1-2%, compared with Apple making roughly 15 pence of profit per £100 of sales. With such a thin margin, you will not attract many equity investors, so the business must run on volume and on debt (27:00-29:30; Slide 22).

The other two are different stories. Tesco's remaining ~75% is mainly suppliers it has not yet paid, plus some loans and bonds. Schroders barely borrows: it manages clients' money *off* its own balance sheet, so clients' billions never become its debt (Slide 22; 30:02). The lecturer generalises: service firms such as consultancies also have small balance sheets. That wafer-thin ~5% cushion is what makes a bank the fragile one — 'that is next week's whole story' (Slide 22). *(Slide 21; Slide 22; 27:00; 28:30; 30:02)*

**Key points:**

- Tesco ~25% equity, Lloyds ~5% equity, Schroders ~65% equity (Slide 22).
- For a bank, 'the debt IS the business': ~95% is deposits and borrowed money (Slide 22).
- Banks work on very thin margins (the lecturer says ~1-2%), so the model depends on volume and debt funding (28:30).
- An asset manager keeps clients' money off its own balance sheet, so it stays light and safe (30:02).

**Examples:**

- Apple's profit margin ~15% vs a bank's ~1-2% margin, used to explain why equity investors would not fund banking (28:00-29:00).
- A consulting firm: few assets, small liabilities, mainly funded by partners/equity (31:00).

## 7. The great choice: debt or equity

The right-hand side of the balance sheet is a dilemma with two very different contracts (Slide 7).

**Debt (a loan)** is a promise to repay with interest. It is a *fixed claim*: lenders get a set amount, and they get paid first. It must be serviced — miss a payment and you can be forced under. Lenders get no ownership, no vote and no share of the upside. Examples: a bank loan, a bond.

**Equity (ownership)** is a share of the company itself. It is a *residual claim*: owners get what is left, and they get it last. There is no obligation to pay anything, and dividends are optional — the lecturer stresses that a CFO can simply say 'this year is tough, no dividend', because there is no contractual obligation. In return, equity holders get ownership, votes and all the upside. Example: ordinary shares in Apple (Slide 7; 35:58-37:00).

The class also discussed the practical side. If you believe strongly in your own business, debt keeps all the upside for you, while bringing in equity holders means sharing it. But two questions follow: *can* you get debt (a bank will ask to see assets, and a start-up has only a promise of future assets), and what if the interest rate is high? That is why many start-ups end up raising equity, for example on Dragons' Den (32:00-35:00; Slide 10). *(Slide 7; Slide 10; 32:00; 35:58; 36:30)*

**Key points:**

- Debt = fixed claim, paid first, must be serviced, no ownership or upside (Slide 7).
- Equity = residual claim, paid last, no obligation to pay, full ownership and all the upside (Slide 7).
- Dividends are optional, not contractual (36:30).
- Start-ups often cannot borrow because banks want assets as security, so they sell equity instead (33:30).

**Examples:**

- A bank loan or a bond (debt) vs ordinary shares in Apple (equity) (Slide 7).
- A founder on Dragons' Den: take a loan, or give away equity for the cash? (Slide 10).

## 8. Capital structure: the CFO's biggest decision

**Capital structure** is simply the mix of debt and equity a firm uses to fund itself (Slide 10). Give away equity and you dilute your ownership forever; take on debt and you owe fixed payments whatever happens. Every firm, from a start-up to Tesco, is choosing a point on this dial. There is no free lunch, only a trade-off (Slide 10).

In the class poll the popular answer was 'a mix of both', and the lecturer agreed: usually the optimum is a combination, because each source brings its own benefits. He says the number one question for any CFO is how much debt to take on, and that you will study this properly in a second-year corporate finance module (around 38:00-40:00; Slides 9, 10).

The mix is not random — it is a conscious decision. The lecturer lists the drivers: interest rates, the firm's strategy, its maturity and growth, shareholders' preferences, and whether it can borrow at all. His example: Microsoft is seen as a more mature business with lower growth and lots of cash flow, and has repaid much of its debt, while Apple was investing heavily a few years ago when interest rates were around 2% — and at 2%, almost anyone would prefer debt (41:00-45:00). Banks are a special case: they have no real choice, because borrowing is their business (40:00). *(Slide 9; Slide 10; 38:00; 41:00; 45:00)*

**Key points:**

- Capital structure = the mix of debt and equity used to fund the firm (Slide 10).
- More debt can make good years great for owners and bad years fatal (Slide 10).
- The optimum is usually a mix, not 100% of either (39:00).
- Drivers mentioned: interest rates, strategy, maturity, growth, shareholder preferences, ability to borrow (45:00).
- Banks are an exception: heavy debt funding is the nature of the business (40:00).

**Examples:**

- Microsoft (mature, lower growth, roughly balanced funding) vs Apple (heavy investment, borrowed when rates were ~2%) (42:12-45:00).
- Poll: 'A profitable firm needs £10m to grow — how should it raise it?' (Slide 9).

## 9. Leverage: the amplifier, in numbers

**Leverage** is the amplifying effect of debt on the owners' returns. Slide 12 compares two identical firms, each with £100m of assets.

Firm A is all equity: £100m equity, no debt. Firm B is half debt: £50m equity + £50m debt at 5%, so £2.5m of interest every year.

*Good year, profit £10m:* A earns 10 ÷ 100 = **10%**. B earns (10 − 2.5) ÷ 50 = **15%**.
*Bad year, profit £2m:* A earns 2 ÷ 100 = **2%**. B earns (2 − 2.5) ÷ 50 = **−1%**, a loss.

The measure used here is **ROE (return on equity) = profit ÷ owners' equity**. Why divide by 50 and not 100 for Firm B? Because we are measuring the return to *equity holders only*, and they put in just £50m. The lenders funded the other £50m but take only their fixed £2.5m — whatever is left belongs to the owners (Slide 12; 46:00-50:00).

Same firm, same profits: debt widens the outcome in both directions. In the good year leverage lifts the owners' return; in the bad year the fixed interest 'kills it all'. The answer to the poll on Slide 11 is therefore C: bigger in good years, worse in bad years (Slides 11, 12; 50:30). *(Slide 11; Slide 12; Slide 26; 46:00; 48:11; 50:30)*

**Key points:**

- ROE = profit ÷ owners' equity (Slide 12).
- Good year: A 10%, B 15%. Bad year: A 2%, B −1% (Slide 12).
- You divide by equity only, because ROE measures the return to owners; lenders take a fixed amount (Slide 12; 48:11).
- Leverage magnifies returns AND losses — the fixed interest obligation does not shrink in a bad year.
- Poll answer: more debt makes owners' returns bigger in good years and worse in bad years (Slide 11; Slide 26).

**Examples:**

- Firm A (all equity) vs Firm B (half debt at 5%, £2.5m interest), good year £10m profit and bad year £2m profit (Slide 12).

## 10. Two kinds of risk: business risk vs financial risk

The lecture separates two sources of risk (Slide 13).

**Business risk** is the risk in the business itself: will people buy the sausage rolls (or the smartphones, or the cereal)? Will costs jump? How is demand or demographics changing? It comes with the industry; you largely *inherit* it when you choose to enter that business, and you can only manage it so far.

**Financial risk** is the *extra* risk you add by financing with debt — the fixed payments you must meet. In the leverage example it was the £2.5m of interest that turned a small profit into a loss. This risk is entirely your choice: it is the dial the CFO and management set (Slide 13; 51:00-53:00).

The lecturer flags these as two terms to remember, and notes that your textbook and MyLab exercises include questions on them (51:30). *(Slide 13; Slide 25; 51:00; 52:00)*

**Key points:**

- Business risk comes from the product, service and industry — largely inherited.
- Financial risk comes from capital structure and fixed debt payments — deliberately chosen.
- Debt increases your riskiness as a business (36:00).
- Both terms are examinable (Slide 25, point 4).

**Examples:**

- 'Will people buy the sausage rolls? Will costs jump?' as business risk (Slide 13).
- The £2.5m fixed interest in the leverage example as financial risk (Slide 13; 52:00).

## 11. How much debt is optimal? Modigliani-Miller, tax shield and distress

Slide 14 answers the 'how much debt' question in three steps.

**Step 1 — M&M I, the base case (1958):** in a perfect world with no taxes and no bankruptcy, how you split debt and equity does not change the firm's value. Same pie, different slices. The lecturer frames this the way finance theories generally work: they build a *benchmark*, and then you study the deviations from it. He compares it to the efficient market hypothesis, which we know is not literally true but is still a useful reference point (Slide 14; 53:55-56:30).

**Step 2 — the tax shield (value UP):** interest is tax-deductible. Slide 14's numbers: borrow £100m at 5% = £5m of interest; at a 25% tax rate that saves £1.25m a year. The lecturer explains the mechanism clearly: a business counts interest as an expense and pays tax only on what is left, so more debt means less tax. He contrasts this with a UK personal mortgage, where you pay tax first and then your mortgage from what remains (57:00-59:00).

**Step 3 — distress costs (value DOWN):** too much debt raises the probability of bankruptcy, and that expected cost eats into value. Borrow too much and the interest can exceed your profit. Net the two frictions and you get a **sweet spot**: the debt level that maximises value (Slide 14; 1:01:30).

Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller later won the Nobel Prize (Modigliani 1985, Miller 1990) (Slide 14). *(Slide 14; 53:55; 57:00; 1:00:30; 1:01:30)*

**Key points:**

- M&M I: in a perfect world (no taxes, no bankruptcy) capital structure does not affect firm value (Slide 14).
- Tax shield: interest is tax-deductible, so debt lowers the tax bill and lifts value — £100m at 5% with 25% tax saves £1.25m a year (Slide 14).
- Distress cost: too much debt raises bankruptcy probability and destroys value.
- Together they create an optimal debt level (a sweet spot).
- Finance theories act as benchmarks you measure reality against (54:30).

**Examples:**

- Business interest treated as an expense before tax vs a UK personal mortgage paid out of after-tax income (58:00).
- Apprenticeship spending made tax-deductible as another example of government using tax rules to encourage behaviour (1:00:30).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The lecturer's answer to 'why does the system encourage debt?' — that tax-deductible interest encourages investment and growth, not just bank profits — is his own argument about policy. It is a reasonable view, but it is an interpretation of the rule rather than a technical result of M&M.

## 12. Zoom out: the world is loading up on debt — and the bill arrives

Slide 15 shows US non-financial corporate debt (bonds + loans) roughly **tripling since 2000**, reaching the mid-teens in $ trillions. ## 13. Refinancing risk: the real question is 'can they roll it over?'

Debt must be repaid — except perpetuals, bonds that never mature and simply pay interest forever. But in practice most big borrowers, governments and large firms, never really repay: they issue new debt to pay off the old. That is **refinancing** (Slide 18).

So the real danger is **refinancing risk**: can the borrower roll the debt over, and at what interest rate? If lenders lose confidence, even a perfectly sound borrower can fail to refinance and collapse. The lecturer uses a familiar comparison: when your fixed mortgage period ends you look to refinance, maybe moving from one bank to another; you may fail if your asset value has fallen or if rates are now much higher (1:05:53-1:07:30).

The case is **Thames Water, 2024-2025**: Britain's biggest water company, with millions of paying customers, nearly sank under about £20bn of debt it could not refinance, and survived only on emergency creditor lifelines (Slide 18). The bottom line: 'a borrower rarely dies because it must repay. It dies because it cannot refinance.' This also links back to last week — when rates rise, rolling over old debt suddenly costs far more — and forward into banks, money and liquidity in weeks 3 and 4 (Slide 18). *(Slide 18; 1:05:53; 1:06:30; 1:07:00)*

**Key points:**

- Most large borrowers roll debt over rather than repay it (Slide 18).
- Refinancing risk = the risk of not being able to roll debt over, or only at a much higher rate.
- Loss of lender confidence can kill an otherwise sound borrower.
- Perpetual bonds never mature and only pay interest (Slide 18).
- A lot of corporate debt matures in the coming years, so refinancing needs are large (1:07:00).

**Examples:**

- Thames Water: ~£20bn of debt it could not refinance, saved by emergency creditor lifelines (Slide 18; 1:07:00).
- A household mortgage at the end of its fixed period, shopping between HSBC and Barclays (1:06:30).

## 14. When it goes wrong: the cash-flow waterfall

If a company collapses, the order of payment is fixed (Slide 17):

1. **Senior lenders** (secured banks and bonds) — paid FIRST. 'Secured' means the loan is backed by assets, so if you do not repay, they take the assets (1:09:30).
2. **Mezzanine / junior debt** — paid next, if anything is left.
3. **Preference shares** — after all debt.
4. **Ordinary shareholders (owners)** — LAST. This is why equity is called the 'first loss'.

Why do the middle layers exist? Senior debt is cheap but limited; equity is expensive. Mezzanine or junior debt ranks behind senior lenders, so it is riskier and pays a higher rate, and it lets a firm borrow more than banks alone would allow — common in buyouts. The lecturer says these lenders (often private equity or investment funds) demand less asset security but charge more, maybe 8-9%, which means the borrower must be sure its investments earn more than that (Slide 17; 1:09:30-1:12:30). **Preference shares** are a debt-equity hybrid: a fixed dividend, paid before ordinary shares, usually with no vote; they raise cash without adding debt or losing control. In 2008 Warren Buffett bought $5bn of Goldman Sachs 'prefs' paying 10% a year (Slide 17).

The case is **Carillion, 2018**: the UK construction giant collapsed owing billions; shareholders were wiped out completely, and even many lenders recovered only about a penny per pound (Slide 17). *(Slide 17; Slide 25; 1:09:30; 1:11:42)*

**Key points:**

- Payment order: senior lenders → junior/mezzanine debt → preference shares → ordinary shareholders (Slide 17).
- Equity is the 'first loss' and is paid last (Slide 25, Slide 26).
- Senior debt is secured on assets, which is why start-ups without assets struggle to get it (1:09:30).
- Junior/mezzanine debt takes more risk and demands a higher interest rate (~8-9% in the lecturer's example).
- Preference shares: fixed dividend, ranked above ordinary shares, usually no vote (Slide 17).

**Examples:**

- Carillion 2018: shareholders wiped out, many lenders recovered ~1p per £1 (Slide 17).
- Warren Buffett's $5bn of Goldman Sachs preference shares paying 10% in 2008 (Slide 17).

## 15. So why would anyone buy equity?

Equity is last in the queue, so why buy it? Slide 19 gives three reasons.

**Limited liability:** you can lose what you put in, but no more — your other assets are safe. **Unlimited upside:** a lender's return is capped at the interest, while equity owns all the growth, forever. **Cash returns:** owners share profits through dividends and share buybacks.

In the recording the lecturer stresses the upside: if you lend, the most you get is the agreed return; if you own a stake, a rising share price gives you capital gains, plus dividends on top. A dividend simply means part of the profit is credited to shareholders' accounts (1:13:00-1:16:30).

The scale is real: Apple has bought back over $700bn of its own shares in a decade and approved around another $100bn in 2025; Shell and BP return billions every year. Dividends and buybacks are how the upside actually reaches the owners — the reward for standing last in the queue (Slide 19). *(Slide 19; 1:13:00; 1:15:00)*

**Key points:**

- Limited liability: your loss is capped at what you invested (Slide 19).
- Unlimited upside: debt's return is capped; equity owns all future growth (Slide 19).
- Owners are rewarded through capital gains, dividends and buybacks (Slide 19; 1:15:00).

## 16. Who stands between: agency vs principal intermediaries

How does the money actually reach the company? Through middlemen — and you classify them by their balance sheet too (Slide 20).

An **agency** intermediary (a broker) introduces the two sides; the asset passes straight between them. The deal never lands on the broker's *own* balance sheet, so the broker takes no risk on it, and earns a fee or commission. Examples on the slide: a stockbroker such as Hargreaves Lansdown, a corporate-finance adviser, a P2P platform. The comparison used is an estate agent: it sells the house but never owns it (Slide 20).

A **principal** (transforming) intermediary stands in the middle as a counterparty, taking the asset onto its own balance sheet and transforming it. A bank turns your deposit (its liability) into a loan (its asset). A market maker buys the share onto its own books and resells it, keeping the bid-ask spread. An insurer takes your premium and your risk: the promise to pay is its liability, and the premiums are invested as its assets. Each one bears risk and earns the spread rather than a simple fee (Slide 20).

The test is not 'does it have a balance sheet' — every firm does. It is: **does the DEAL sit on its own balance sheet?** Broker no, transformer yes (Slide 20). The asset manager from Slide 22 fits this logic: clients' money is invested off its own books, so it never becomes its debt (30:02). *(Slide 20; Slide 22; Slide 25; Slide 28; 30:02)*

**Key points:**

- Agency/broker: introduces parties, takes no balance-sheet risk, earns a fee or commission (Slide 20).
- Principal/transformer: takes the asset onto its own balance sheet, bears the risk, earns a spread (Slide 20).
- The test: does the deal sit on the intermediary's own balance sheet? (Slide 20).
- Bank, market maker and insurer are all transformers (Slide 20).

**Examples:**

- Hargreaves Lansdown or a P2P platform as a broker; 'like an estate agent: sells the house, never owns it' (Slide 20).
- A bank turning your deposit into a loan; a market maker keeping the bid-ask spread; an insurer investing premiums (Slide 20).

## 17. Where balance sheets meet: primary and secondary markets

The poll on Slide 23 asks: you buy a Tesco share on your phone — who gets your money? The answer is **B, the investor who sold you the share**. Tesco already received its money when the shares were first issued (Slide 23; 1:17:25).

That is the difference between the two markets (Slide 24). In the **primary market**, brand-new securities are sold to raise fresh money, which funds real investment: IPOs, new bond issues, rights issues, underwriting. This is 'funding liquidity' — cash actually reaches the company. In the **secondary market**, existing securities change hands between investors, and the company receives nothing new; this is everyday trading on the LSE or NYSE, with market makers quoting prices. This is 'market liquidity' — you can sell whenever you want.

The sizes show how different they are. Slide 24: about $30tn of bonds and $0.6tn of shares newly issued worldwide in 2025, against roughly $161tn of bonds and $158tn of shares already out there (SIFMA, 2025). The lecturer's point is that students often think the primary market is where the money is, but the trading market is far bigger (1:19:00).

The link back: every bond and share on the balance sheets we drew was issued once in the primary market and has traded in the secondary market ever since. And the debt-versus-equity choice — the Modigliani-Miller question — is decided at the moment the firm issues here (Slide 24). *(Slide 23; Slide 24; Slide 25; 1:17:25; 1:19:00)*

**Key points:**

- Primary market = new securities, new money for the firm (IPOs, bond issues, rights issues) (Slide 24).
- Secondary market = existing securities traded between investors; no new money for the firm (Slide 24).
- Buying a Tesco share today pays the seller, not Tesco (Slide 23; 1:17:25).
- The secondary market is far larger than the primary market (Slide 24; 1:19:00).
- Primary = 'funding liquidity'; secondary = 'market liquidity' and price discovery (Slides 24, 25).

**Examples:**

- Slide 24's case: SpaceX, 12 June 2026, described as the largest IPO in history — about $75bn raised at a valuation above $2 trillion, with the stock up ~19% on day one; the $75bn is the primary market, every trade since is the secondary market.
- An IPO where the company 'rings the bell' at the New York Stock Exchange or NASDAQ (1:18:30).

## How the ideas connect

- Last week's discounting toolkit tells you what a security is worth; this week's balance sheet tells you who owns it and who funded it — the same securities appear as assets on one balance sheet and liabilities on another (Slides 2, 8).
- The accounting equation (Slide 4) creates the debt-vs-equity question (Slide 7), because the right-hand side must be filled by either lenders or owners.
- The debt-vs-equity choice leads directly to leverage (Slide 12): dividing by a smaller equity base is exactly why debt amplifies the owners' return.
- Leverage explains financial risk (Slide 13), and financial risk is the 'distress cost' that limits the tax shield in the M&M framework (Slide 14).
- The tax shield explains the macro picture: it is one of the three reasons given for the tripling of US corporate debt (Slides 14, 15).
- High debt plus higher interest rates since 2022 produces refinancing risk (Slide 18), which produces rising defaults (Slide 16), which brings you to the waterfall of who gets paid (Slide 17).
- Equity's last place in the waterfall (Slide 17) is the exact reason it owns all the upside (Slide 19) — risk and reward are two sides of one contract.
- The ~5% equity of a bank (Slide 22) is explained by the bank's role as a transforming (principal) intermediary turning deposits into loans (Slides 8, 20), and sets up next week's lecture on bank fragility (Slide 27).
- Capital structure is not just theory: the debt/equity decision is physically executed in the primary market when the firm issues bonds or shares (Slide 24).
- Primary vs secondary markets explains the Tesco poll (Slide 23) and also explains 'market liquidity', which is why investors are willing to hold securities at all (Slide 24).

## Recap

1. A balance sheet always obeys assets = liabilities + equity: the left side is what the firm owns, the right side is who funded it, and equity = assets − liabilities is the cushion that decides solvency.
2. Apple FY2025 shows this in practice: $359bn of assets = $285bn liabilities + $74bn equity, so a $3tn company runs on only about 20% owners' equity; 'current' means within a year and 'non-current' means longer.
3. The whole financial system is linked balance sheets — your deposit is the bank's liability, the bank's loan is the company's liability — so every asset is somebody else's liability.
4. There are only two ways to fund anything: debt is a fixed promise paid first with no upside, and equity is a residual, optional-payment claim with ownership and all the upside.
5. Leverage magnifies the owners' return in both directions: with half debt at 5%, ROE rises from 10% to 15% in a good year but falls from 2% to −1% in a bad year, because the interest must be paid anyway.
6. Business risk is inherited from the industry, while financial risk is deliberately chosen through the capital structure; M&M says the mix is irrelevant only in a perfect world, and in reality the tax shield lifts value while distress costs cap it, creating an optimal debt level.
7. Cheap money, the tax shield and buybacks tripled US corporate debt, but since 2022 higher rates have raised refinancing risk and roughly doubled default rates — Thames Water nearly failed because it could not roll over about £20bn.
8. When a firm collapses the waterfall runs senior lenders → junior/mezzanine debt → preference shares → ordinary shareholders, which is why equity is the first loss (Carillion), and intermediaries are classified by whether the deal sits on their own balance sheet (broker no, transformer yes — banks run on only ~5% equity); new money is raised in the primary market, while the much larger secondary market only transfers existing securities between investors.
