# IC101 L03 — Explanation

> [!info] Пошаговое объяснение (сгенерировано). Лекция: [[IC101 L03 — Commercial, Investment and Central Banks]]. Ссылки *(Slide N; MM:SS)* ведут к источнику — сверяйся с ними.

## Overview

This lecture follows the 'life and death' of a bank in one story. It starts with what a bank legally is (an institution licensed to take deposits, which means it also creates most of the country's money), then explains the 'magic trick' of turning short, safe, small deposits into long, risky, large loans, why that makes a bank fragile, how a bank run happens (Northern Rock 2007, Silicon Valley Bank 2023), and what safety net stops runs (capital, liquidity buffers, deposit insurance, the central bank). From these answers the three types of bank fall out: commercial, central and investment banks. It matters because the thin equity cushion (~5% in the simple example) explains both why banks are special and why they collapse, and because Slide 29 says the mid-term multiple-choice test is built from these points.

## 1. Where we start: last week's fragile balance sheet

Lecture 2 'X-rayed' a bank's balance sheet. A normal company like Apple is funded with about 20% equity, a retailer 25–40%, but a bank runs on only about 5% equity. In other words, a bank is almost entirely funded with **other people's money** (deposits). Slide 2 says this thin cushion is exactly why banks are special and why they collapse.

This week answers three questions in order: (1) what does a bank actually DO with your money? (2) why does it break, and what is a bank run? (3) who stops it breaking? The three types of bank then come out of those answers. Slide 2 states that SVB (2023) and Northern Rock (2007) 'both died the same way'.

The lecturer opened with exactly that idea: a bank takes £100bn of deposits, lends most of it out long-term for mortgages and loans, so if everyone turns up at once the money 'is simply not available'. In the long run the loans do pay back (monthly cash flows), but not today. *(Slide 2; Slide 3; 00:00)*

**Key points:**

- A bank is funded with ~5% equity; normal companies 20–40%.
- Deposits = other people's money, repayable on demand.
- The lecture is one story: what a bank does → why it breaks → who stops it → the three bank types.
- Northern Rock 2007 and SVB 2023 are the two running examples.

**Examples:**

- Apple ~20% equity, a retailer 25–40%, a bank ~5% (Slide 2).
- A bank with £100bn of deposits cannot repay them all today because the money is lent out long-term (00:00).

## 2. What a bank really is: the licence to take deposits

Legally, a bank is an institution with a **licence to take deposits**. Your deposit is your asset but the bank's **liability** — the bank owes you that money. Slide 4 adds the strange part: those deposits ARE the country's money; about 97% of all money is just bank deposits, not coins and notes.

The lecturer explained the licence with a practical example: without a licence you are only a payment platform. Revolut existed for years as a payment/FX app and only got its banking licence recently. Without a licence you effectively cannot lend the deposits out — you must keep most of the funds ready for withdrawal. With a licence you may lend the money out, but you must follow the rules (how much equity, how much cash you keep ready) and you come inside the government guarantee scheme.

Why banks are called 'special' (Slide 4): £1 in the bank is always worth £1 (**fixed value**); you can take it out any time (**on demand**); banks run the payments system (cards, transfers, salaries); and they provide the credit that 'lubricates' the economy. That is why they are licensed and watched so closely. Scale figures on Slide 4: ~$190tn of global banking assets, ICBC the world's biggest bank at ~$6tn, and 4 of the 5 biggest banks are Chinese — in class a student suggested this is because of China's huge population and very large savings/deposits. *(Slide 4; 00:00; 05:54)*

**Key points:**

- Bank = licensed deposit-taker; no licence = payment platform only.
- Your deposit is your asset but the bank's liability.
- ~97% of all money is bank deposits, not cash.
- Bank money is fixed in value and available on demand — that is the promise.
- Banks also run payments and supply credit.

**Examples:**

- Revolut: a payment app for years, bank licence only recently (00:00).
- You can walk into HSBC and demand all your money, and the bank must pay (00:00).
- ICBC (China) is the world's biggest bank, ~$6tn (Slide 4).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The link between 'licence' and 'guarantee' is the key idea to remember: permission to use depositors' money comes together with rules and public protection. That is the deal society makes with banks.

## 3. The magic trick: maturity and risk transformation

Slide 5 shows the core of banking as a transformation. Your deposits are **short** (withdrawable any day), **safe** (fixed £ value) and **small** (many savers). The bank turns them into loans that are **long** (a 25-year mortgage), **risky** (some borrowers will not repay) and **large** (one big borrower).

There are two transformations. **Maturity transformation** = borrow short, lend long. The bank profits from the gap but must always find cash for today's withdrawals. The lecturer made the 'gap' concrete: a savings account might pay you around 2.5% (an instant-access current account can be close to 1%), while a new mortgage costs roughly 5.5%. That margin is the bank's income. **Risk transformation** = each single loan is risky, but spread across thousands of loans the **law of large numbers** makes the pool predictable: some of you will not repay, most will. The lecturer compared it to insurance companies, which know roughly how many fire claims they get each year — although good years and bad years still differ with the economy.

Slide 5 notes the historical origin: the goldsmiths of 17th-century London invented this by lending out deposited gold and keeping only a fraction in reserve. In the lecture this was called **fractional reserve banking**: you lend most of the deposits and keep only a fraction, because people normally only ask for 10–20% back at any time. Slide 5's bottom line: this transformation is what a bank IS, and exactly why it is fragile — 'it lives on a promise it cannot instantly keep'. *(Slide 5; 05:54; 11:59)*

**Key points:**

- Short, safe, small deposits → long, risky, large loans.
- Maturity transformation: borrow short, lend long; profit = the interest margin.
- Risk transformation: pooling many loans makes losses predictable (law of large numbers).
- Fractional reserve banking: keep a fraction, lend the rest.
- The fragility is built into the business model, not a mistake.

**Examples:**

- Savings ~2.5% (current account near 1%) vs mortgage ~5.5% — the margin (05:54).
- Insurance: the insurer knows how many claims arrive each year (11:59).
- 17th-century London goldsmiths lending out deposited gold (Slide 5).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> A useful way to remember it: the bank sells 'liquidity' (instant access) to savers and 'patience' (long-term money) to borrowers, and earns the difference. The risk is that it has sold more instant access than it can physically deliver on one day.

## 6. How bank lending creates money (and what limits it)

Slide 9 states the mechanism simply: a loan adds money, moving money between banks does not, and repaying a loan removes money. Banks do not print money; they create it by lending and cancel it when loans are repaid.

The lecturer's worked example: suppose there is £1,000 in people's bank accounts. Someone asks for a £200 loan. The bank types £200 into the borrower's account in its ledger. The original £1,000 is still there — nobody's deposit fell to £800 — so now there is £1,200. If the borrower spends the £200 or moves it to another bank, the banks settle with each other, but the total stays £1,200. Only when the borrower **repays** does the £200 disappear and the total go back to £1,000. So the money in the economy is the running total of lending minus repayments.

It is not unlimited. Slide 9 lists the limits: you need **creditworthy borrowers**, the loan must be **profitable**, **capital rules cap it** (the slide says 10% equity), and the cash needed to settle payments costs the **base rate**. That is why the cost of borrowing is how the central bank steers the money supply: lower rates → more borrowing → more money created. The lecturer added the warning example: if lending were unlimited you get 2008, when banks lent recklessly for mortgages to people who were not creditworthy, together with practices such as securitisation. *(Slide 9; 17:59; 1:06:03)*

**Key points:**

- New loan = new deposit = new money (a ledger entry).
- Transfers between banks do not change the total money.
- Repayment destroys money.
- Limits: creditworthy borrowers, profitability, capital rules, cost of settlement cash.
- The central bank steers money indirectly through interest rates, not by printing.

**Examples:**

- £1,000 of deposits + a £200 loan = £1,200; repayment takes it back to £1,000 (17:59).
- 2008: reckless mortgage lending to non-creditworthy borrowers as the result of lending without limits (17:59).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> This is probably the single most counter-intuitive idea of the lecture. It helps to see that the bank's new loan is an asset and the new deposit is a liability, so the balance sheet still balances — the bank created both sides at once.

## 7. Why banks are fragile: fixed promises, variable assets

Slide 10 states the fragility in one line: fixed promises on one side, assets that can fall on the other. Deposits are fixed in value and payable on demand; loans can lose value when borrowers default.

The lecturer walked through the arithmetic. A bank has £100 of deposits, £5 of equity, and £105 of loans as assets. If the loans lose about 6% of their value, the asset side falls by roughly £6.3, which is more than the £5 of equity — so equity goes to about minus £1.3. **Negative equity means insolvent**: the bank owes more than it owns and could not repay all depositors if asked.

He added an important nuance about ordinary companies: because a balance sheet is only a snapshot on one day, a normal business can show negative equity on that date without disaster (it may be about to sell or buy assets). For a bank, **consistent** negative equity is a real problem, precisely because of the maturity mismatch: the £100 of deposits is fixed and due on demand. *(Slide 10; 24:11)*

**Key points:**

- Liabilities are fixed and on demand; assets are long and variable.
- Loss > equity ⇒ negative equity ⇒ insolvent.
- A ~5% cushion is wiped out by a ~6% fall in asset value.
- A balance sheet is a one-day snapshot; for banks persistent negative equity is the danger signal.

**Examples:**

- £100 deposits + £5 equity, loans fall 6% ⇒ equity about −£1.3 (24:11).

## 8. Pricing credit risk: expected loss = PD × LGD × amount

Banks cannot know *which* loans will go bad, but from historical data they know roughly *how many* will. Slide 11 gives the formula: **expected loss = PD × LGD × amount**, where **PD** (probability of default) is the chance the borrower fails and **LGD** (loss given default) is the share you lose if they do.

Worked example from the slide: a £1,000 loan, PD 2%, LGD 40% ⇒ expected loss = 2% × 40% × £1,000 = **£8**. In class the lecturer asked why LGD is 40% and not 100%. The answer given by a student and confirmed: loans are often **secured** against an asset — if you stop paying your mortgage, the bank takes the house. He also mentioned debt collection agencies as another way lenders recover some money, with a personal story about an agency coming to take furniture when his housemate did not pay the bills.

The key idea (Slide 11, quoting Scott-Quinn Ch. 8): the bank builds that £8 into the interest rate, so **expected** losses are paid out of revenue. It is the **unexpected** losses — a bad year worse than the average — that eat into equity. The lecturer summed up: apart from that, a bank is a very safe business; the only two real risks are a much-worse-than-average year, or everyone demanding their money back at once. *(Slide 11; Slide 30; 24:11)*

**Key points:**

- Expected loss = PD × LGD × loan amount.
- LGD is below 100% mainly because loans are secured (collateral) and some debt is recovered.
- Expected losses are priced into the interest rate and paid from revenue.
- Unexpected losses hit equity.
- Practise the formula: Slide 30 gives 3% × 50% × £1,000 = £15.

**Examples:**

- £1,000 loan, PD 2%, LGD 40% ⇒ expected loss £8 (Slide 11, 24:11).
- Mortgage default: the bank takes the house, so it does not lose everything (24:11).
- Exam-style: £1,000 loan, PD 3%, LGD 50% ⇒ £15 (Slide 30).

## 9. Poll: who absorbs the losses? (equity first — and don't forget revenue)

Slide 12 asked: a bank has £100 of deposits and £5 of equity and its loans fall 6% in value — what happens? The slide's loss waterfall (Slide 13) repeats Lecture 2's order: expected losses come out of revenue, unexpected losses come out of equity, and with only ~5% equity 'it does not take much'.

In the lecture the lecturer called this a **trick question**. Looking only at the slide's numbers, the answer is that equity is wiped out and the bank is insolvent (Slide 12 option B). But the simple picture leaves out the bank's **revenue**. He said that once you include the profit the bank is making that year, a 6% loss still leaves roughly £1 of equity, so the bank can absorb it; a small 2% loss would simply reduce that year's profit and 'nobody would notice'. With a 10% loss, however, equity is wiped out completely and depositors are short by about £3 — the bank is insolvent and cannot repay all deposits.

The practical lesson: when you do these calculations, remember the order — revenue first, then equity, then depositors. *(Slide 12; Slide 13; 29:56)*

**Key points:**

- Loss waterfall: revenue → equity → depositors.
- The slide's simple version: 6% loss on a 5% cushion = insolvent.
- The lecturer's fuller version: revenue is an extra buffer before equity.
- A ~10% loss definitely wipes out equity and reaches depositors.

**Examples:**

- 2% loss: absorbed by this year's profit, only analysts notice (29:56).
- 10% loss: equity gone, depositors short about £3 (29:56).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> For the mid-term, the safest approach is to use the order given on Slide 13 (revenue, then equity) and read the question carefully: if no revenue is mentioned, treat equity as the only cushion.

## 10. The bank run: how a sound bank dies in a day

Slide 14 defines the run: everyone wants their money at once, and it simply is not there. The lecturer described the self-fulfilling chain: a rumour or an announcement that the bank needs more capital → depositors withdraw → the bank sells loan portfolios or other assets quickly to raise cash → those fire-sale losses eat the equity → equity turns negative → panic spreads → more withdrawals.

**Northern Rock, 2007**: within about three days, after the bank asked for emergency help, people withdrew about £2bn; there were queues outside branches; the bank was eventually nationalised and the government guaranteed the deposits.

**SVB, 2023** (Slides 15 and 16): the run happened on phones, in hours. $42bn left on the Thursday and about another $100bn was queued by Friday noon — Slide 16's headline is '$142bn tried to leave in 36 hours'. Slide 15 says this was about 85% of deposits (the lecturer said 80%), sparked on group chats and X. The trigger, as the lecturer explained, was SVB selling bonds quickly at below their original value ('fire selling'), which made people ask why, followed by the bank announcing it needed more capital. Slide 15 adds that 2023 saw more bank assets fail than in 2008, and that regulators are rewriting rules 'for the smartphone age' because deposits now flee faster than the rulebook ever assumed. *(Slide 14; Slide 15; Slide 16; 29:56; 36:11)*

**Key points:**

- A run is self-fulfilling: fear → withdrawals → forced asset sales → real losses → more fear.
- Northern Rock 2007: days, physical queues, £2bn withdrawn, later nationalised.
- SVB 2023: hours, $42bn in one day, ~$142bn attempted in 36 hours, ~80–85% of deposits.
- Social media and mobile banking made runs much faster.
- 2023 involved more failed-bank assets than 2008.

**Examples:**

- SVB's fire-sale of bonds as the trigger for the rumour (29:56, 47:59).
- The government closed SVB, guaranteed the deposits and removed the reason to run (36:11).

## 11. Illiquidity vs insolvency — the decision that matters

Slide 18 draws the distinction that decides whether a bank is saved or left to fail. **Illiquid**: the bank is sound but cannot find cash today; its assets are good but locked up long while depositors want cash now. This is a cash-flow problem, and a loan can save it. **Insolvent**: the bank is genuinely bust — it owes more than it owns because its loans went bad; this is a balance-sheet problem, and a loan only delays the end. Slide 18 quotes Tim Geithner: telling the two apart is 'critical and very hard to do'.

The poll on Slide 17 asked which failing bank the central bank should rescue. Most students chose the illiquid one. The lecturer confirmed the rule: a central bank or government will move in to protect an illiquid bank; the rule says you should **not** protect the insolvent one that made terrible loans. But in practice **systemic risk** often forces governments to rescue even badly run banks, and that creates **moral hazard** — if banks know a safety net exists, they have an incentive to take bad risks.

He classified SVB as **illiquid, not insolvent**. Its problem was growth, not recklessness: deposits from profitable tech start-ups grew much faster than its lending business (it was restricted to the start-up community and could not easily write mortgages), so it put the extra money into bonds — supposedly the low-risk choice — just as the bond market was falling sharply. His lesson: if deposits grow and you cannot lend them, keep them safe rather than investing in assets that can lose value. *(Slide 17; Slide 18; 41:56; 47:59)*

**Key points:**

- Illiquid = timing problem, savable with a loan.
- Insolvent = value problem, a loan only delays failure.
- The rule: rescue the illiquid, not the insolvent.
- Systemic risk often overrides the rule → moral hazard.
- SVB was illiquid: a growth and bond-market problem, not reckless lending.

**Examples:**

- 'If I spread a rumour that Barclays has an issue, Barclays will have a problem' — any bank can be made illiquid (41:56).
- SVB's deposits grew faster than its loan book, so it bought bonds (47:59).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The lecturer's SVB story highlights interest-rate risk: when rates rise, existing fixed-rate bonds fall in price. That link is explained on Slide 22 in the QE context but was not spelled out as 'this is why SVB's bonds lost value' — connecting the two is my reading, not a direct statement.

## 12. The safety net: layers built after each crisis

Slide 19 shows the layers a shock has to pass through before it reaches your deposit: **supervision and rules** (Bank of England — spot trouble early); **capital**, the equity cushion (the slide says 10–12% of risk-weighted assets) which absorbs the loss first; **liquidity buffers**, cash kept ready to meet a rush of withdrawals; **deposit insurance** via the FSCS (Financial Services Compensation Scheme), so there is no reason to run; and the **central bank** as **lender of last resort**, the final backstop. The slide notes most shocks are absorbed by the first layers; the last ones exist for rare, severe runs.

The lecturer explained each layer. The rules come from the **Basel** framework (named after the Swiss city where regulators meet); we are now at Basel 3 with Basel 3.1 under discussion. Capital includes shares issued and, mostly, **retained earnings** (profits kept inside the bank). **Risk-weighting** means each asset is weighted by how risky it is: a loan carries more risk weight than an office building, so the ratio is expressed against risk-weighted assets rather than all assets.

He called deposit insurance 'the most clever of all': because the government guarantees your deposit, you have no reason to queue, so the run does not start and the government almost never actually has to pay. He noted that someone with £500,000 might still panic, since only the guaranteed amount is covered. Finally the central bank can lend short-term for a fee — as happened in the SVB case. *(Slide 19; 53:43)*

**Key points:**

- Five layers: supervision → capital → liquidity buffers → deposit insurance → lender of last resort.
- Capital rules come from the Basel framework (now Basel 3, Basel 3.1 debated).
- Capital is measured against risk-weighted assets, not total assets.
- Deposit insurance works psychologically: it prevents the run rather than paying for it.
- Large depositors above the limit still have a reason to run.

**Examples:**

- FSCS deposit guarantee removes the incentive to queue (53:43).
- A loan has a higher risk weight than an office building (53:43).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> SVB is a good illustration of the limit of deposit insurance: its depositors were mostly companies with balances far above any guarantee, which is one reason the run was so fast. The lecture mentions large uninsured depositors in general terms but does not make this exact point about SVB.

## 13. Have the fixes worked? Safer, but never 'safe'

Slide 20 shows big banks' core equity capital (CET1) rising from roughly the mid single digits in 2007 to around 12% in 2013 and about 14% by 2024 — the slide says the cushion 'roughly tripled' since before the crisis. After 2008 the Basel rules forced banks to hold far more equity, plus liquidity buffers and stress tests, so a bank today is genuinely much harder to topple than in 2007.

But Slide 20 also says it is not solved: mid-sized banks and the new **speed** of runs are the frontier, and the rules are still being fought over — Basel 3.1 tightening while others push to loosen. Its bottom line: 'Safer than 2007, but never safe. The argument over how much capital is enough never ends.'

The lecturer explained the politics. Banks and financial firms lobby for less regulation; regulators lobby for more. A student gave the reason banks want lower equity requirements: they can use more of the money and increase profitability. The lecturer added the other side: with very high equity requirements, returns to shareholders fall, so it becomes harder to attract equity investors at all. Governments, meanwhile, want to guarantee less, because nationalising a bank in a crisis is 'a complete public relations disaster'. *(Slide 20; 59:54)*

**Key points:**

- Core equity capital roughly tripled since 2007; big banks now hold ~14%.
- Post-2008 reforms: more capital, liquidity buffers, stress tests.
- 2023 showed the problem is not solved: mid-sized banks and run speed.
- Basel 3.1: an ongoing fight between tightening and loosening.
- Higher equity = safer bank but lower return on equity, so banks resist it.

**Examples:**

- Banks lobby for lower requirements so they can lend/deploy more and raise profitability (59:54).

## 14. The central bank: what the Bank of England does

Slide 21 is titled 'seven jobs, one institution'. The lecturer said he had simplified the textbook treatment and went through these roles:

1) **Monetary policy** — a committee votes on the policy interest rate (Bank Rate). He stressed a subtle but important point: when the central bank cuts rates it is not printing money; more people borrow, and the **commercial** banks create the money by crediting loans (the £1,000 → £1,200 example again). 2) **Issuing the currency** — the physical notes, each carrying the Bank's promise, though that is only ~3% of money. 3) **Lender of last resort** — short-term emergency lending, the final backstop. 4) **Regulating and supervising banks** — checking they hold enough capital and cash, with the power to stop a weak bank from lending further or paying dividends to shareholders. 5) **Custodian of the foreign exchange reserves** — he gave the figure $190bn held in dollars, needed because the UK pays for things like oil in dollars and must be able to service dollar debt if export inflows stop. He added the risk that reserves held in foreign banks can be frozen — about $300bn of Russian assets were frozen after the war in Ukraine started, because they sat in London and New York — which is why central banks also keep physical **gold** in their vaults as a reserve they fully control. 6) **Settlement** — banks settle with each other through accounts at the central bank (for example when you move your mortgage to another bank). *(Slide 21; 1:06:03)*

**Key points:**

- Monetary policy: setting Bank Rate by committee vote.
- Rate cuts work indirectly: cheaper borrowing → commercial banks create more money.
- The central bank issues notes, regulates banks and is lender of last resort.
- It holds FX reserves (and gold) so the country can always pay abroad.
- It provides the accounts in which banks settle payments with each other.

**Examples:**

- ~$190bn of UK dollar reserves, needed to pay for oil in dollars (1:06:03).
- ~$300bn of Russian reserves frozen because they sat in London and New York banks (1:06:03).
- Gold in the Bank of England's vaults can physically be flown abroad (1:06:03).

## 15. Monetary policy in action: rates, QE and QT

Slide 22 describes the recent cycle: rates went from 0.1% to 5.25% to break 11% inflation and are now coming back down. The lecturer walked through the graph: during COVID inflation fell below the 2% target because people lost jobs and demand collapsed, so the central bank cut rates to almost zero to stimulate demand; afterwards inflation peaked, so rates had to rise sharply; as inflation came back towards 2%, rates were cut again.

The natural question he asked the class: what do you do when the rate is already near zero and demand is still weak? Answer: **quantitative easing (QE)**. Slide 22 explains it carefully: the Bank creates new money and uses it to buy government bonds from pension funds and other big investors. The huge new demand pushes bond prices up, and because a bond pays a fixed amount, a higher price means a lower interest rate (£5 a year on £100 is 5%; on £125 it is 4%). Since mortgage and company borrowing rates are priced off the government bond rate, borrowing gets cheaper across the whole economy. The lecturer described the mechanics bluntly: the Bank credits new money into the fund's account and takes the bond — so there are fewer bonds in the market and new money in the economy.

**Quantitative tightening (QT)** is the reverse: the Bank sells the bonds back or lets them mature, and the money comes out of the system. Slide 22's framing: monetary policy covers both the interest rate and the money used to buy or sell bonds — but, as the lecturer said, 'the main lever is the interest rate', and you can never control the balance exactly. *(Slide 22; 1:12:20)*

**Key points:**

- Bank Rate is the main lever; QE is the extra tool when rates are near zero.
- QE = create money, buy government bonds from big investors.
- Bond price up ⇒ yield (interest rate) down; other borrowing rates follow.
- QT = sell bonds or let them mature, removing money from the system.
- Policy is inexact: keeping rates near zero for long contributed to later high inflation.

**Examples:**

- £5 a year on a £100 bond = 5%; the same £5 on £125 = 4% (Slide 22).
- COVID: demand collapsed, rates cut to near zero, then QE (1:12:20).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The price–yield relationship on Slide 22 is also the key to SVB: when rates rose from 0.1% towards 5.25%, existing low-interest bonds fell in price, which is how a 'safe' bond portfolio produced large losses. The slides do not join these two points explicitly.

## 16. Three banks, one system

Slide 23 compares the three institutions along five lines. **Takes your deposits?** Commercial: yes, its core business. Investment: no. Central: no, only the banks' accounts. **Creates money?** Commercial: yes, the ~97%. Investment: no. Central: yes — cash plus reserves, the monetary base. **Its customers:** commercial — you and businesses; investment — companies, governments, big investors; central — the banks and the government. **Inside the safety net?** Commercial: yes, insured, with the lender of last resort behind it. Investment: no, normally outside it. Central: it IS the safety net. **Regulated?** Commercial: heavily, with a full banking licence. Investment: a lighter licence. Central: it is the regulator.

Slide 23 then gives the decisive historical evidence: in 2008 Lehman Brothers, a pure investment bank, sat outside the net and was allowed to fail, while Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley became **bank holding companies overnight** to get inside the central bank's protection.

The lecturer added two nuances. First, investment banks do sometimes hold some deposits, but at the commercial/business level, not retail. Second, on Lehman: in hindsight US officials might say they should have stepped in, because once Lehman went down the systemic risk threatened Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley too — which is exactly why those two were brought inside the net. He summarised that governments tend to protect the banking system 'irrespective', which is the source of moral hazard. *(Slide 23; 47:59; 1:18:23)*

**Key points:**

- Only commercial banks take retail deposits and create most money.
- Investment banks have a lighter licence and sit outside the safety net.
- The central bank is the regulator and the safety net itself.
- Lehman failed outside the net; Goldman and Morgan Stanley converted to get inside it.
- Protecting the system creates moral hazard.

**Examples:**

- Lehman Brothers, 2008 — allowed to fail (Slide 23, 47:59).
- Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley became bank holding companies overnight (Slide 23, 1:18:23).

## 17. The other bank: what an investment bank is

Slide 24 describes the investment bank as 'a different animal': no retail deposits, so no classic bank run, and a lighter licence. Its clients are not you but companies, governments and big investors. It does not lend from a deposit base; it **connects issuers and investors in the capital markets**. Named examples: Goldman Sachs, JP Morgan, Morgan Stanley.

It has **two faces**: a **corporate-facing** side that helps companies and governments **raise** money and do deals, and an **investor-facing** side that helps funds and big investors **buy, sell and trade** those securities. Standing between the two sides of the market is the classic **sell side** — as the lecturer put it, they are the middlemen/agents, as opposed to the **buy side** (funds that invest money).

Slide 24's bottom line: no deposits means no classic run, but its risks live in the markets rather than on a high street. *(Slide 24; 1:18:23)*

**Key points:**

- No retail deposits ⇒ no classic bank run, lighter licence.
- Clients: companies, governments, large investors.
- Two faces: corporate-facing (raise money, do deals) and investor-facing (trade securities).
- It is the 'sell side'; funds are the 'buy side'.
- Its risk sits in markets, not in deposits.

**Examples:**

- Goldman Sachs, JP Morgan, Morgan Stanley (Slide 24).
- The bank stands between an issuer who needs money and investors who have money (1:18:23).

## 18. Where the money really comes from: Goldman Sachs' four engines

Slide 25 uses Goldman Sachs, 2025, to show the four revenue engines. **Raising capital = underwriting**: taking a company public or issuing its bonds, and guaranteeing the sale for a fee. **M&A advice**: advising on buying and selling companies (the lecturer notes 'this is my world'). **Sales & trading**: buying and selling securities for clients (**agency**) and with the bank's own money (**principal**). **Asset management**: running clients' money for a steady fee.

The surprise on the slide: the glamorous deal fees are the **smallest** engine at $9.3bn, while trading ($31bn) and managing money ($17bn) pay most of the bills. The lecturer gave total 2025 revenue as about $58bn and said sales and trading is the main business for most investment banks. He described asset and wealth management as advising very wealthy individuals or companies with spare cash on long-term investments and building portfolios for them — paid by a steady fee rather than trading commission.

He also unpacked the deal side (the 'investment banking department', IBD). **Equity underwriting**: if a company lists its shares, the bank advises and **underwrites** — it guarantees the shares will be sold, effectively buying them and reselling them into the market. The same happens for **bonds**. **M&A advisory**: companies grow either organically (building a new plant or product themselves) or by acquiring a ready-made company; the bank advises the buyer on what to acquire, or advises a seller and finds a buyer, earning a commission. *(Slide 25; 1:24:05)*

**Key points:**

- Four engines: underwriting, M&A advice, sales & trading, asset management.
- Trading ($31bn) and asset management ($17bn) dominate; deal fees are smallest ($9.3bn).
- Agency trading = for clients; principal trading = with the bank's own money.
- Underwriting means guaranteeing the sale of new shares or bonds.
- M&A: advise the buyer or the seller and earn a commission.

**Examples:**

- Goldman Sachs 2025: ~$58bn total revenue, IBD only $9.3bn (Slide 25, 1:24:05).
- 'If I am Apple, Goldman Sachs advises me on what company to buy next' (1:24:05).

## 19. Careers: so you want to be an IB analyst?

Slide 26 covers a day on the desk and six skills to start building this year. The lecturer spoke about this at length because it is a common career goal. His honest summary: graduate programmes in investment banking pay more than almost anything else, but it comes with a big price — he said it offers 'zero work–life balance', with analysts still working at 11pm, midnight or 1am, and roughly double the hours of other jobs. **Examples:**

## 20. Conflicts, Chinese walls, and the moving line between the two bank types

Slides 27 and 28 close the story, but note that the lecturer ran out of time: he said 'we're going to stop here because there's another lecture after us' and that he would send a message about the remaining couple of slides, which he called 'a concept that is not as relevant'. So what follows is from the slides only.

**Slide 27 — conflicts and walls.** The same bank advises a company, trades for investors, and bets its own capital. Whose side is it on? It could misuse secret client information to trade for itself. Principal trading is powerful but risky and 'helped sink banks in 2008'. The controls are: **'Chinese walls'** — strict information barriers between the deal-advising and trading sides; separation of **front / middle / back office** (deal-makers, risk controllers, settlement); and regulators watching for insider dealing and abuse. Bottom line: 'one roof serving all sides only works while the walls hold'.

**Slide 28 — the wall built, torn down, and 2008.** 1933: **Glass-Steagall** legally separates commercial and investment banking in the US after the 1929 crash — deposits must not fund risky trading. 1999: **Gramm-Leach-Bliley** repeals it, creating 'universal banks' doing both under one roof. 2008: the reckoning — risky investment-banking losses threaten deposit-taking giants and taxpayers bail them out. Now: **ring-fencing** again walls off everyday deposits from risky trading. The eternal question: how far should everyday deposits fund risky markets? The argument is live right now — tighten (Basel 3.1) versus loosen. *(Slide 27; Slide 28; Slide 29; 1:30:20)*

**Key points:**

- Universal banks face structural conflicts of interest.
- Chinese walls and front/middle/back-office separation are the controls.
- Principal trading contributed to 2008 losses.
- 1933 Glass-Steagall → 1999 repeal → 2008 crisis → today's ring-fencing.
- The core policy question: should deposits fund risky markets?

**Examples:**

- Glass-Steagall (1933) and its repeal by Gramm-Leach-Bliley (1999) (Slide 28).

## How the ideas connect

- The licence to take deposits (Slide 4) is what makes money creation by lending possible (Slide 9): deposits are the money, and a new loan creates a new deposit.
- Maturity transformation (Slide 5) is both the source of profit (the interest margin) and the source of fragility (Slide 10) — the same feature produces both.
- The ~5% equity cushion from Lecture 2 (Slide 2) is what turns a ~6% fall in asset value into insolvency (Slides 12-13), which explains why capital rules now target 10-12% of risk-weighted assets (Slide 19).
- Expected vs unexpected loss (Slide 11) explains the loss waterfall (Slide 13): priced-in losses are paid from revenue, surprises are paid from equity.
- Insolvency and illiquidity (Slide 18) connect the arithmetic of Slide 13 to the run on Slide 14: a rumour of insolvency causes withdrawals, and forced asset sales can turn an illiquid bank into a genuinely insolvent one.
- Deposit insurance (Slide 19) works because the run is self-fulfilling (Slide 29, point 5): remove the reason to run and the run never starts, so the guarantee rarely has to be paid.
- Interest rates and QE (Slide 22) are the central bank's way of steering the money that commercial banks create (Slide 9) — the central bank changes the price of borrowing, banks do the creating.
- The bond price/yield relationship in QE (Slide 22) is the same mechanism that made SVB's 'safe' bond portfolio lose value when rates rose (47:59).
- The safety net (Slide 19) defines the three bank types (Slide 23): commercial banks are inside it, investment banks outside it, and the central bank IS it — which is why Lehman failed while Goldman and Morgan Stanley converted to get inside.
- Conflicts of interest inside universal banks (Slide 27) are the reason the legal wall between the two bank types keeps being built and torn down (Slide 28), and that debate links straight back to how much capital is enough (Slide 20).

## Recap

1. A bank is an institution licensed to take deposits, and those deposits are about 97% of all the money in the economy.
2. The 'magic trick' is transformation: short, safe, small deposits become long, risky, large loans, and the bank earns the interest margin between them.
3. Commercial banks create money when they lend and destroy it when loans are repaid; limits come from creditworthy borrowers, profitability, capital rules and the cost of settlement cash.
4. Banks are uniquely fragile because liabilities are fixed and payable on demand while assets are long and can fall in value, on a cushion of only about 5% equity in the simple example.
5. Credit risk is priced with expected loss = PD × LGD × amount (2% × 40% × £1,000 = £8); expected losses come out of revenue, unexpected losses out of equity.
6. A bank run is self-fulfilling and now extremely fast: Northern Rock took days in 2007, while SVB saw $42bn leave in a day and ~$142bn attempted in 36 hours in 2023.
7. The crucial judgement is illiquidity (sound assets, no cash today — savable with a loan) versus insolvency (owes more than it owns — a loan only delays the end).
8. The safety net has layers — supervision and Basel rules, capital, liquidity buffers, FSCS deposit insurance and the central bank as lender of last resort — and the three bank types are defined by their place in it: commercial inside, investment outside, central bank as the net itself.
