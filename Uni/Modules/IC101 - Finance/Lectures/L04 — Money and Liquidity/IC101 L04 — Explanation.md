# IC101 L04 — Explanation

> [!info] Пошаговое объяснение (сгенерировано). Лекция: [[IC101 L04 — Money and Liquidity]]. Ссылки *(Slide N; MM:SS)* ведут к источнику — сверяйся с ними.

## Overview

This lecture asks two simple-sounding questions that run through the whole of finance: what is money, and what is liquidity? It shows that money is defined by three jobs (medium of exchange, store of value, unit of account), that modern money is 'fiat' money backed only by trust, that most money is actually digital bank deposits sitting in a 'pyramid' of promises, and that liquidity — how fast and cheaply you can turn something into cash — is what really kills banks and funds. It then looks at the future of money: the decline of cash, Bitcoin, stablecoins and a possible digital pound. It matters because almost everything later in the course (valuation, risk, banking, markets) depends on these two building blocks.

## 1. Where we start: the £10 note puzzle

The lecture begins by linking back to Lecture 3. Last week the module kept using the word 'money' without defining it: deposits *are* money, and banks create money when they lend. It also said that a bank run is usually a **liquidity** crisis, not an insolvency crisis — the bank's loans were fine, but too many people asked for cash at the same time and the cash was not there.

The professor then holds up a £10 note. It says: *'I promise to pay the bearer on demand the sum of ten pounds.'* He asks who promises this and what it means. A student answers that the central bank promises it — but you already hold the £10, so what is actually being promised? The answer is that this sentence is a **historical artefact**: in the past the note said it would pay you the sum in **gold**, and the central bank had to hold gold in its vaults to honour that. That is no longer true.

So the puzzle is: a £10 note costs a few pence to make, is not backed by gold, and the government will not swap it for anything valuable. Why will anyone give you £10 of real goods for it? *(Slide 2; 00:00; 05:53)*

**Key points:**

- Recap from Lecture 3: deposits are money; banks create money by lending; a bank run is usually a liquidity crisis, not insolvency.
- The 'I promise to pay the bearer' line is left over from the gold era.
- If you take a £10 note to the Bank of England today, they will only give you another £10 note — not gold.
- The real question of the lecture: what gives money its value?

**Examples:**

- A student says the note is valuable because 'it's physical' and you can buy a bottle of water with it — and you know the shop will accept it.

## 2. The answer is trust, not gold and not the law

The first poll (Slide 3) asks what makes a £10 note worth £10: (A) gold in a vault, (B) the government will swap it for something valuable, (C) we all simply believe and agree it is worth £10, or (D) the cost of the paper and ink. The correct answer is **C**.

The professor rules out the others one by one. There is no gold backing — that ended in the 1970s, and the gold held at the Bank of England does not correspond to the money in circulation. The government will not swap the note for anything valuable. The law does matter a little (it is legal currency because the central bank issued it), but the law is not what makes it work. What makes it work is **trust**: you trust that the note is worth £10, and you trust that the next person has no real option but to accept it.

The slide calls this 'a shared social contract' — money works only because we all believe it does. The professor stresses that this trust idea is the key to everything else in the lecture. *(Slide 2; Slide 3; 05:53)*

**Key points:**

- Poll answer: C — we all believe and agree it is worth £10.
- Not gold: the gold link was cut in the 1970s.
- Not a government swap promise: there is nothing to swap it for.
- Trust is 'fragile and powerful' — it is the shared belief that holds money together.
- Reminder: a £10 note is part of only ~3% of all money; 97% sits in bank accounts.

**Examples:**

- If you demand gold for your tenner at the central bank, 'they're just going to give you another ten'.

- The professor says a digital pound (or digital dollar) is coming 'in a few years' time, maximum'.

## 4. What is money? The three jobs

Money is defined by **what it does**, not by what it is made of. Slide 5 gives the three jobs:

1. **Medium of exchange** — everyone accepts it, so you can swap it for anything and you do not need to barter. The professor's example: a baker and a shoemaker no longer have to trade bread for shoes; both accept the same tenner.
2. **Store of value** — it holds its worth over time, so you can save today and spend next year. If you leave £100 in your bedroom, you know that in six or twelve months nothing dramatic will have happened to its value. (Inflation nibbles at it, but slowly.)
3. **Unit of account** — it is the common 'ruler' we price everything in: a coffee is £3, a car is £30,000. We do not price a car as '1,000 bottles of water'.

If something does all three jobs, it is money. Strange things have passed this test in the past, simply because a community agreed to trust them: gold and silver coins, cigarettes in WWII prisoner-of-war camps, cattle, and cowrie shells. *(Slide 5; Slide 6; 08:00; 12:10)*

**Key points:**

- Three jobs: medium of exchange, store of value, unit of account.
- Money is defined functionally — by its jobs, not its material.
- Pass all three jobs = it is money.
- A £10 note and a bank deposit clearly pass all three.

**Examples:**

- Cigarettes in German POW camps: prisoners received cigarettes regularly, so even non-smokers used them to buy other goods — an accepted currency inside the camp.
- Cowrie shells were used as official currency in West Africa (the professor says even into the 20th century) and much earlier in places like China and India. When German traders found huge numbers of the shells elsewhere and brought them in, the supply rose, the value collapsed, and people stopped using them.
- Cattle as money — the professor notes this was 'a pretty inefficient form of money'.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The cowrie-shell story is a neat early lesson about money supply: if the quantity of a money rises faster than the goods you can buy with it, its value falls. That is the same mechanism behind inflation later in the lecture.

## 5. Running the three-job test on real candidates

The professor then tests several things against the three jobs — this is a technique you can reuse in the exam.

**Gold** today: it is *not* a medium of exchange (you cannot pay for your shopping with gold) and almost nothing is priced in gold, so it fails two of the three tests. **Bitcoin**: not really a medium of exchange (few shops take it), a poor store of value (it is very volatile — you might hold the equivalent of $1,000 this year and $100 next year), and nobody prices a coffee in it. So it fails all three, and behaves like a speculative asset.

**Stablecoins** are introduced briefly here. A student calls them 'virtual dollars' / 'crypto dollars', and the professor accepts this: you put down one dollar for every stablecoin you buy, and someone holds that dollar for you while you hold a digital token. He says they are getting closer to being a medium of exchange, with more and more things priced in them. Finally, even cigarettes in the POW camps were an imperfect store of value: when bombing stopped the deliveries, cigarette 'prices' moved; when deliveries increased or food deliveries slowed, their value fell. So the answer to 'is X money?' often depends on the situation. *(Slide 5; Slide 6; 12:10)*

**Key points:**

- Gold fails two tests today (not a medium of exchange, not a unit of account).
- Bitcoin fails all three — it is a speculative asset, not money.
- Stablecoins are backed 1-for-1 by a dollar held in reserve; they score better but are not yet a full medium of exchange.
- Even a working money (cigarettes) can lose its store-of-value job when supply or demand shifts.

**Examples:**

- 'You can't walk into a grocery store and pay with gold these days.'
- Bitcoin's volatility: priced in dollars, your holding can fall sharply within a year.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> Notice the structure here: Bitcoin is judged *against the dollar*. The fact that we measure Bitcoin's value in dollars is itself evidence that the dollar, not Bitcoin, is doing the unit-of-account job.

## 6. Money is not currency: 3% cash, 97% deposits

This is a recap from Lecture 3 but it is essential. Only about **3%** of money is cash (notes and coins). The other **97%** is **bank deposits** — numbers on a bank's computer. Together these make **broad money**, roughly £3.2 trillion in the UK.

The professor explains the term **IOU** ('I owe you'). Your bank deposit is an IOU of a **commercial bank**: the banks collectively owe depositors about £3.2 trillion. **Narrow money** is the money that really 'exists' as central-bank money: the notes and coins plus the **reserves** that commercial banks hold at the Bank of England. The rest is a claim on a bank, not a thing.

Where did the £3.2 trillion of deposits come from? Mostly from commercial banks **lending**: each new loan creates a new deposit. Some came from the Bank of England's **quantitative easing** — the central bank creates new money and uses it to buy government bonds, which puts that money into circulation and raises the money supply.

The crucial comparison: a £10 note is an IOU of the **central bank**; £10 in your account is an IOU of a **commercial bank** (for example Barclays). That is a weaker promise — which is why, if people fear their bank is in trouble, they rush to convert deposits into cash. Deposit guarantee schemes exist, but the underlying promise is still a private one. *(Slide 7; 18:27)*

**Key points:**

- Cash ≈ 3% of money; bank deposits ≈ 97%.
- Broad money ≈ £3.2 trillion in the UK.
- Narrow money = notes and coins + commercial banks' reserves at the Bank of England.
- IOU = 'I owe you'. A deposit is a bank's debt to you.
- Deposits are created mainly by bank lending; some money comes from QE (central bank creates money and buys government bonds).
- Cash = central-bank IOU (safer); deposit = commercial-bank IOU (less safe).

**Examples:**

- 'Each new loan creates a new deposit.'
- The difference between the tenner in your pocket and £10 in your Barclays account is whose promise it is.

## 7. The history: commodity money → gold standard → 1971 → fiat money

Slide 8 tells the story of how money cut its last link to gold, in four stages:

- **Ancient: commodity money.** The money *was* something valuable — gold and silver coins worth their weight. If you melted the coin down you got the metal's value, which is why it was simple to trust.
- **1800s–1971: the gold standard.** Paper notes circulated, but each one could be swapped at the bank for a fixed amount of gold. Central banks were required by law to hold gold corresponding to the money in circulation.
- **1971: Nixon cuts the link.** The United States ended gold convertibility. Since then money has been backed by nothing but trust.
- **Today: fiat money.** 'Fiat' is Latin for 'let it be' — 'let it be money'. It is money because the state says so and because we all believe it.

Why did the gold link end? A student gives the reason the professor accepts: gold is **finite**. You cannot mine as much gold as you want, so if economies are to grow and more money is to be created, gold becomes a straitjacket. The professor hints at a second reason and says he will return to it, but in the transcript he does not clearly come back to it.

The bottom line on Slide 8: **when trust dies, money dies** — Zimbabwe printed a 100-trillion-dollar note; Venezuela and 1920s Germany did the same kind of thing. The professor adds the thought experiment: if the government announced tomorrow that cigarettes are the official money, people would either take a very long time to accept it or simply not trust it, and it would fail. *(Slide 8; Slide 11; 18:27; 24:08)*

**Key points:**

- Four stages: commodity money → representative (gold-backed) money → end of convertibility in 1971 → fiat money.
- 'Fiat' = 'let it be'; fiat money is money because the state says so and we believe it.
- Main stated reason for ending the gold link: gold is finite and limits the growth of money and the economy.
- Trust cannot simply be ordered into existence by the state.

**Examples:**

- Zimbabwe's 100-trillion-dollar note as a symbol of lost trust.
- The 'cigarettes as official currency' thought experiment.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> Background (not stated in the sources): under a gold standard a country also loses freedom to set its own interest rates and exchange rate, because it must defend convertibility. That is a common second reason for abandoning gold, and it may be the point the professor planned to return to — but the sources do not say this, so treat it only as a possibility.

## 8. Inflation: the quiet tax

Because fiat money rests on trust and can be created, it leaks value. Slide 9 calls inflation a **quiet tax**. Inflation means prices rise, so each £1 buys a little less every year. The Bank of England **targets 2%**, so money is *designed* to leak slowly.

**Who pays:** anyone holding cash, or lending at a fixed interest rate. Your savings account has lost buying power every year since 2021. **Who gains:** borrowers — and the biggest borrower is the state. Inflation shrinks the national debt in real terms 'without a vote or a bill'. That is why it is 'quiet': nobody has to pass a tax law.

The numbers: UK inflation spiked to **11.1% in October 2022**; £100 in 2020 buys about £80 of goods today. The slide's 'deeper read' notes inflation back down to **2.9%** in the 12 months to July 2026 (ONS).

Why target 2% and not 0%? The professor works this out with the class. With 0% inflation, interest rates would sit at about 0%, and then the central bank has almost no room to cut rates to stimulate the economy. Quantitative easing can help a little, but it is much weaker than the interest-rate tool. His example is **Japan**, which had roughly zero inflation and zero interest rates for years, could not cut further (you cannot easily go negative), and struggled to grow — until inflation finally picked up and Japan could raise rates again. So 2% comes from a model: enough of a cushion to keep the interest-rate tool usable. *(Slide 9; 24:08)*

**Key points:**

- Inflation = prices rising, so money loses buying power; the BoE target is 2%.
- Losers: cash holders and fixed-rate lenders. Winners: borrowers, above all the government.
- It is 'quiet' because it reduces real debt without any political decision.
- UK CPI peaked at 11.1% in October 2022; ~2.9% in the 12 months to July 2026.
- A 2% target keeps interest rates above zero so the central bank still has room to cut.

**Examples:**

- £100 in 2020 buys about £80 of goods today.
- Japan: near-zero inflation and zero rates left the central bank with no room to stimulate the economy.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The zero-lower-bound argument the professor makes is the standard textbook justification for a positive inflation target; a second common argument (not in the sources) is that mild inflation makes it easier for firms to adjust real wages without cutting anyone's cash pay.

## 9. When trust dies: hyperinflation in three cases

Slide 10 shows what happens when the same mechanism gets an unlimited dose: more money chasing the same goods. The professor goes through three historical cases.

**Germany, 1923.** Germany had to pay war reparations after the First World War, had striking workers demanding wages, and not enough foreign currency. So it printed — to pay reparations and to pay salaries. The more it printed, the less the currency was worth, so even more was needed. At the peak, prices doubled in about 3.7 days. People burned pine cones because they were cheaper than wood; a loaf of bread cost a wheelbarrow of cash. It ended only with a new, more stable currency, a stop to printing, and postponed reparations.

**Zimbabwe, 2008.** The government seized farms — the main source of export income — and gave them to people affiliated with the government who did not know how to run them, so output fell to roughly 30–40% of capacity. Foreign income collapsed, so there were no dollars to pay for imports. But the state still had to pay public servants and pensions, so it printed. Inflation went to extreme levels in a single month (November 2008).

**Venezuela, 2018.** The oil price fell and oil output collapsed because of corruption and inefficient management. Oil income was the state's main revenue, so again the state printed to fund its deficits — reaching around 1,000,000% inflation.

Slide 10 sets out the common four-step mechanism: (1) foreign income collapses, so there are no dollars, no imports, and the shelves empty; (2) only one tap is left — tax revenue falls with the economy and nobody will lend, so wages are paid by printing; (3) same wages but fewer goods, so prices rise and next month's wage bill is bigger, so the state prints more; (4) everyone dumps the currency — people spend it the day they get it or buy dollars, and money stops doing all three jobs.

The bottom line: **nobody chooses hyperinflation**. A state with no exports, no tax base and no lenders chooses to pay this month's wages by printing — and then chooses it again every month. The cure is a central bank that can say no, which is why the Bank of England is independent. *(Slide 10; 24:08; 30:23)*

**Key points:**

- One mechanism, three countries: collapse of foreign income → printing to pay wages → spiral.
- Germany 1923: reparations + strikes + no foreign currency; prices doubled roughly every 3.7 days at the peak.
- Zimbabwe 2008: farm seizures destroyed export income; state printed to pay the public payroll.
- Venezuela 2018: oil price and oil output collapsed; printing to fund deficits; ~1,000,000% inflation.
- Final stage: people dump the currency and money fails all three jobs.
- The brake is an independent central bank.

**Examples:**

- Burning pine cones instead of wood in 1920s Germany.
- A wheelbarrow of cash for a loaf of bread.

## 10. Why central bank independence matters

The professor explains what independence actually means. The government cannot order the central bank to set interest rates to its liking, and cannot order it to print more money through quantitative easing. The decision-making committee is appointed through the state, but by law it can decide without being influenced by government. If it leaked that a government official had tried to influence the central bank, that government could fall over it.

His live example: the current UK government would like lower interest rates, because cheaper mortgages make voters happier — but that could let inflation get out of control. Governments sometimes act opportunistically and dislike high interest rates. In Germany, Zimbabwe and Venezuela, by contrast, the government effectively decided that the central bank must print. Independence is the mechanism that lets the central bank say no. *(Slide 10; 36:22)*

**Key points:**

- Independence = the government cannot dictate interest rates or money creation.
- Independence is the institutional 'brake' against hyperinflation.
- Politicians have short-term incentives to want low interest rates.

**Examples:**

- A UK government wanting rate cuts so mortgages are cheaper and voters are happier.

## 11. The family tree of money: five types

Slide 11 arranges the types of money in order of how abstract they are:

1. **Commodity money** — valuable in itself: gold and silver coins ('specie').
2. **Representative money** — paper claims on a commodity; the old gold-backed note. The professor notes that with the end of the gold era we essentially do not have representative money anywhere today.
3. **Fiat money** — backed only by trust and the state; today's cash and coins.
4. **Electronic / bank money** — digital deposits, about 97% of all money; numbers on a bank's computer. The professor points out students may not have realised that most money is electronic 'because it doesn't really exist' as a physical thing.
5. **Near-money and e-money** — things easily turned into cash: PayPal, M-Pesa, wallets, and new crypto claimants.

The trend is clear and worth remembering: money keeps getting **more abstract** — from gold you can hold, to a paper claim on gold, to pure trust, to a number in a database, to a line of code. *(Slide 11; 36:22)*

**Key points:**

- Five types: commodity, representative, fiat, electronic/bank, near-money/e-money.
- 'Specie' = metal coins valuable in themselves.
- Representative money is essentially gone today.
- The long-run direction: gold → claim → trust → number → code.

**Examples:**

- PayPal balances and crypto accounts as examples of near-money / e-money.

## 12. The money pyramid: not all 'money' is equally safe

Slide 12 shows the same idea as a pyramid with real UK numbers. At the **base** sits **central-bank money** — notes and coins plus banks' reserves at the Bank of England, about **£745bn**. This is the safest money: it is the state's promise. Above it sits **commercial-bank money** — your deposits, about **£3.2tn** — which is a private bank's promise and can fail, as Lecture 3 showed. At the **top** sits **e-money**: wallets and stablecoins, only tens of billions, which is a *company's* promise resting on the layers below.

The key structural insight: **each layer rests on the layer below**. The safest layer is also the **smallest**. So if everyone tries to move down the pyramid into cash at the same time, there simply is not enough — and that is exactly what a bank run is. The professor notes the slightly odd picture: the base is the foundation of the whole system, yet it is the smallest amount of money.

He also notes the practical reason we do not all hold cash even though it is safest: you cannot safely keep all your money as notes in your room, because it could be stolen. *(Slide 12; 36:22)*

**Key points:**

- Three layers: central-bank money (safest, ~£745bn) < commercial-bank money (~£3.2tn) < e-money (tens of billions).
- Safety falls as you go up; quantity rises as you go up.
- Each layer is a promise resting on the layer beneath it.
- A bank run = everyone rushing down the pyramid into the smallest layer at once.
- Ask of any 'money': whose promise is it?

**Examples:**

- Stablecoins and wallets sit at the top: a company's promise, not a bank's or the state's.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> A useful shortcut for the exam: whenever you meet a new kind of 'money', place it on the pyramid by asking whose IOU it is. That single question organises cash, deposits, e-money, stablecoins and CBDCs.

## 13. Liquidity: the definition and the two things it is NOT

This is the second big idea of the lecture, and the professor says it is the one students find hardest. **Liquidity = how quickly and cheaply you can turn an asset into cash without dropping its price.**

Cash is perfectly liquid — it *is* cash. A house is illiquid: to sell it tomorrow you would have to slash the price; otherwise you might wait one or two months, sometimes six months or a year.

Why it matters: **bills come due in cash, not in assets.** A family, a firm or a bank can own a great deal and still fail to pay on Friday, because what it owns cannot be turned into cash in time. Every bank in Lecture 3 that failed was in that position — long before it ran out of value.

Two things liquidity is **not**:
- **Not wealth.** You can be rich but illiquid, with all your money tied up in property. The professor's example: people who own London property worth billions and face a large additional property tax from April 2027 — they own a lot but may have no cash flow to pay the tax, and the asset takes time to sell.
- **Not solvency.** A bank can be **solvent** (its assets are sound) and still **illiquid** (no cash today). This is 'exactly the killer distinction from Lecture 3'.

And why it matters to you as an investor: liquidity is the hidden second question behind every investment. Not only 'what will it be worth?' but 'can I get out when I need to, and at what price?' The slide's memorable line: liquidity is like air — you never think about it until there is none, and then it is the only thing that matters. *(Slide 13; 42:32)*

**Key points:**

- Liquidity = speed + cheapness of turning an asset into cash without a price cut.
- Cash is perfectly liquid; property is illiquid.
- Bills are paid in cash, not in assets — you can be asset-rich and still default.
- Liquidity ≠ wealth.
- Liquidity ≠ solvency: a sound bank can still fail for lack of cash today.
- For investors: always ask 'can I get out, and at what price?'

**Examples:**

- Selling a house fast means slashing the price; selling at full value may take months.
- Property owners facing a new tax from April 2027 who hold value but not cash.

## 14. The liquidity poll and the liquidity spectrum

The second poll (Slide 14) asks which you could turn into cash **fastest at full value**: (A) a £250,000 flat, (B) £5,000 of shares in a big listed company, (C) £5,000 in your current account, or (D) a £5,000 painting. Based on Slide 15, the current account is the most liquid, with listed shares next; the flat and the painting are far slower. The professor's spoken discussion of the answer is partly garbled in the transcript, but his points are clear: the flat is 'definitely not the right answer'; shares can be liquidated through your trading account with money in your bank the same day or within a couple of days; and the painting depends heavily on how you price it — if you price it below its value you might sell the same day.

Slide 15 then lays out the **liquidity spectrum**, from most to least liquid: **cash** (instant) → **bank deposit** (seconds) → **government bonds** (minutes) → **shares** (days) → **property** (months) → **art / private business** (years). The professor adds a nuance: shares are usually liquid in minutes, but liquidity depends on the stock exchange — some markets are much less liquid.

His most important nuance on the poll: liquidity depends on the **price at which you are willing to sell**. A flat 'worth' £1 million that you try to sell at £1 million is very illiquid; price it low enough and almost anything sells fast. That is why the definition includes 'without dropping its price'. *(Slide 14; Slide 15; 42:32; 48:26)*

**Key points:**

- Spectrum: cash → bank deposit → government bonds → shares → property → art/private business.
- Listed shares in a big company are liquid; a flat or a painting is not.
- Liquidity always depends on the price you accept — speed can be bought with a discount.
- Liquidity also depends on *which* market the asset trades on.

**Examples:**

- £5,000 of shares: sell in your trading account and the money is in your bank the same day or within a couple of days.
- A flat 'worth £1m' that you insist on selling at £1m is highly illiquid.

## 15. The price of liquidity: the liquidity premium and liquidity risk

Liquidity has a price. Less-liquid assets must **pay more** — this extra return is the **liquidity premium**. Slide 15's example: a 10-year gilt (UK government bond) yields **less** than a loan to a small firm, partly because you can sell the gilt in a second. The premium is your reward for tying money up, and your compensation for the risk that you cannot get out when you need to.

The professor explains the same idea from the price side: take two otherwise identical assets, and the more liquid one will have the **higher price**, because people pay for the ability to liquidate whenever they want. In a panic the premium jumps: in March 2020 even the gap between the buying and selling price of a US Treasury bond widened tenfold.

**Liquidity risk** is the official term, and Slide 15 gives a sharp definition: *the gap between what you have promised and what you actually hold.* This is not only a banking problem. A **bank** borrows at the liquid end (instant deposits) and lends at the illiquid end (25-year mortgages). **Funds** do the same: a property fund that promises daily withdrawals owns buildings that take months to sell — which is why UK property funds had to shut their doors to withdrawals in 2016 and again in 2020. The professor places liquidity risk alongside the other risk types the course uses: market risk, business risk, liquidity risk. *(Slide 15; 48:26; 54:43)*

**Key points:**

- Liquidity premium = the extra return you earn for holding a less-liquid asset.
- A 10-year gilt yields less than a loan to a small firm, partly because of liquidity.
- Equivalently: the more liquid of two identical assets trades at a higher price.
- Liquidity risk = the gap between what you promised and what you actually hold.
- Banks and funds both run this gap on purpose — it is their business model.
- In a panic the liquidity premium widens sharply (US Treasury bid-ask gap ×10 in March 2020).

**Examples:**

- UK property funds suspended withdrawals in 2016 and 2020 because daily redemption promises met assets that take months to sell.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The slide's wording ('less-liquid assets pay more') and the professor's wording ('the more liquid asset has the higher price') are two sides of the same coin: for a bond, a higher price means a lower yield. If you are unsure in an exam, use the slide's version.

## 16. When liquidity vanishes: three kinds of run, one mechanism

Slide 16 shows that the same mechanism appears at three levels.

**A bank run.** The bank is solvent and its loans are fine, but it cannot turn them into cash fast enough. The fix: the central bank acts as **lender of last resort** and lends cash against those good loans.

**A market run — March 2020.** When the pandemic was declared, nobody knew how markets or government finances would be affected, so everyone tried to sell (liquidate) everything at once, even **US Treasuries**, normally the safest assets in the world. Only a central bank can create more cash, so the **Fed bought about $3 trillion of bonds in three months** and the market reopened. The professor stresses that this was literally newly created money: the Fed's balance sheet grew by that $3tn within about three months.

**A fund run — September 2022.** UK **pension funds** had to sell gilts to meet **collateral calls** (a requirement to post liquid assets as security). Their selling pushed gilt prices down, which forced more selling — a spiral. The **Bank of England bought £19bn of gilts in two weeks** to stop it. The trigger was the Liz Truss government's 'mini budget' with **unfunded tax cuts** — tax cuts with no explanation of how they would be paid for. Markets concluded the government would have to borrow much more, so gilt prices fell and government borrowing costs rose (the professor mentions a rise of roughly half a percentage point to about two percentage points), making borrowing even less affordable. The Chancellor resigned within days and the Prime Minister about a week later. 'That's what happens when you mess with the markets.'

The bottom line: **liquidity is confidence.** The moment everyone doubts it and reaches for cash together, it evaporates — for banks, funds and whole markets alike. *(Slide 16; 54:43; 1:00:49)*

**Key points:**

- Three runs — bank, market, fund — all driven by everyone reaching for cash at once.
- Only the central bank can create cash, so it is the lender of last resort / buyer of last resort.
- March 2020: even US Treasuries became hard to sell; the Fed bought ~$3tn of bonds in three months.
- September 2022: pension funds' forced gilt selling spiralled; BoE bought £19bn of gilts in two weeks.
- Collateral calls force funds to sell, which can create a self-reinforcing price fall.
- Unfunded tax cuts damaged market confidence and raised government borrowing costs.

**Examples:**

- The 2022 UK 'mini budget': unfunded tax cuts → gilt prices fall → collateral calls → more selling → BoE intervention → resignations.

## 17. The slow death of cash

Slide 17 charts cash as a share of UK payments. Cash fell from **over half of UK payments in 2011 to below 10% today**, and **cards overtook cash in 2017**. Over half of UK adults now use a mobile wallet (Apple Pay, Google Pay). **Sweden** is nearly cashless (the professor says around 2% of payments). **Kenya's M-Pesa** 'put a bank in every phone': people without bank accounts send money to each other by text message, and the professor says around 60–70% of Kenyan transactions now run through that system.

But cash still matters, for three reasons. **System failures:** if a shop's card system is down and nobody has cash, that can create panic — so the economy needs at least some cash. **Vulnerable people:** elderly people who are not comfortable with technology, or people with memory problems, would be badly affected; the professor suggests it would be close to immoral to remove cash completely. **Privacy:** going fully cashless would be a very sensitive political issue, and a government that tried would lose votes.

The professor also notes a banking angle: cash that stays inside the banking system (rather than in people's wallets) can be used by banks to issue more loans. *(Slide 17; 1:00:49)*

**Key points:**

- Cash: over 50% of UK payments in 2011 → under 10% today; cards overtook cash in 2017.
- More than half of UK adults use a mobile wallet.
- Sweden is nearly cashless; Kenya's M-Pesa banks people through their phones.
- Cash still matters for resilience (network/power failures), the vulnerable, and privacy.

**Examples:**

- 'Sorry, cash only — our system is down' — the resilience argument for keeping cash.
- M-Pesa: money sent by text message, no bank account needed.

## 18. What actually happens when you tap?

Slide 18 opens up the 'plumbing' behind a cashless payment, in four steps:

1. **You tap** your card or phone at the till.
2. **Authorise** — the card network (Visa/Mastercard) or Faster Payments checks within seconds that the money is there in your account.
3. **Deposits move** — your bank debits your account and the shop's bank credits theirs. The two bank systems communicate.
4. **Settle** — at the end of the day the two banks settle the **net** difference between them in **Bank of England reserves**. Because there may be millions of transactions between, say, Barclays and HSBC, they are netted and settled together. The slide says the Bank's settlement system moves about **£350bn a day**.

The bottom line is important: **nothing physical moves.** A payment simply reassigns bank deposits, and the banks then settle in central-bank money — the base of the pyramid. That is why the base still matters even as cash disappears. *(Slide 18; 1:07:03)*

**Key points:**

- Four steps: tap → authorise → deposits move → banks settle in central-bank reserves.
- Authorisation goes through the card network or Faster Payments and takes seconds.
- Settlement is net, not transaction-by-transaction.
- A payment is just a reassignment of deposits — no physical money moves.
- The base of the pyramid (central-bank reserves) is what ultimately settles everything.

**Examples:**

- Barclays and HSBC net millions of daily transactions and settle one difference using their reserves at the Bank of England.

## 19. Challenger 1: Bitcoin — money without a bank or a state

Slide 19 frames Bitcoin as a product of the 2008–09 crisis. It was launched in **January 2009**, weeks after the bank bailouts, by someone using the name **'Satoshi Nakamoto'** whose real identity is unknown. The design point is political: **no bank, no state, no central bank that can print**. There is a **fixed supply of 21 million coins**, run by code and a crowd. The professor notes that when it started around 17 million existed, with the rest to be issued up to 21 million.

Now run the three-job test. **Medium of exchange:** few shops take it. **Store of value:** about **$126k in October 2025** and about **$79k by September 2026** — far too volatile. **Unit of account:** nobody prices a coffee in it. **Verdict: it fails all three today.** It behaves like a speculative asset — 'digital gold' — not money.

The real-world test: **El Salvador** made Bitcoin legal tender in 2021 under a young, tech-minded president, and gave people $30 in a wallet to encourage use. People took the free $30 and went back to dollars. The law was dropped in **2025** at the IMF's request. The professor calls this 'the most natural experiment you have', and says it failed because Bitcoin fails all three tests and so cannot earn the trust it needs when its price swings so widely. *(Slide 19; 1:07:03)*

**Key points:**

- Launched January 2009 after the bailouts; creator pseudonym 'Satoshi Nakamoto'.
- Fixed supply of 21 million coins; no state, no bank, no central issuer.
- Fails all three money tests today — especially store of value, because of volatility.
- Best described as a volatile, speculative asset ('digital gold').
- El Salvador's legal-tender experiment (2021) failed and was dropped in 2025.

**Examples:**

- Bitcoin ~$126k (Oct 2025) → ~$79k (Sept 2026): about 37% below the peak.
- El Salvador's free $30 wallets — people spent them and returned to dollars.

## 20. How Bitcoin works: replacing a trusted middleman with a shared record

Slide 20 contrasts two ways of recording a payment.

**The normal way:** you → your bank → your friend. The bank keeps **one private ledger** and checks that you have the money. You must trust the bank to keep that record honestly. The professor points out how deeply we rely on this without noticing: when you pay for an Amazon order, you almost never check whether your bank recorded it correctly — you simply trust HSBC or Barclays.

**The Bitcoin way:** you → the network → your friend. Thousands of computers worldwide each hold an **identical copy** of one public record and race to verify the payment. There is no bank; you trust open code and the crowd instead of an institution.

The **blockchain** is that record: verified payments are sealed into **blocks**, each block locked to the one before, so nobody can rewrite history.

The professor explains the trust problem the design solves. If one person or one computer could decide what goes into the record, you would have to trust that person — and the system could be hacked or that person could lie. So the design makes sure **nobody knows who will write the next block**. Thousands of computers compete to solve a problem set by the code. The problem is so hard that it requires an enormous number of guesses and therefore a lot of electricity. Whoever solves it first writes the next block, which is then sent to all the other computers, which accept it. This process is called **mining**, and the winner receives a reward in Bitcoin. Businesses run data centres with thousands of machines for this; in the early days you could mine with an ordinary computer. The reward must be worth more than the electricity, or mining stops. The professor's conclusion: the expensive, unpredictable competition is exactly *why* the system can be trusted — you cannot attack the system at the moment a block is written, because nobody knows who will write it. *(Slide 20; 1:07:03; 1:13:21)*

**Key points:**

- Banking = one private ledger kept by a trusted institution.
- Bitcoin = one public ledger copied across thousands of computers; trust in code and the crowd.
- Blockchain = blocks of verified payments, each locked to the previous one, so history cannot be rewritten.
- Mining = computers competing to solve a deliberately hard problem and win the right to write the next block, plus a Bitcoin reward.
- The point of the difficulty and the energy cost is unpredictability — nobody knows who writes next, which makes the record hard to attack.

**Examples:**

- You never check whether your bank recorded your Amazon payment correctly — that is institutional trust in action.
- Mining data centres with thousands of machines, spending large sums on electricity for the block reward.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The key idea to carry away is not the technology but the *substitution*: Bitcoin does not remove the need for trust, it moves trust from an institution to a set of rules and economic incentives. That is a fair way to compare it with the money pyramid, where every layer is somebody's promise.

## 21. Challenger 2: stablecoins

Slide 21 presents stablecoins as crypto's answer to its own volatility problem. **What it is:** a crypto token **pegged 1-to-1 to the dollar**. One dollar in, one token out, and the issuer holds that dollar in reserve — in Treasury bills and bank deposits. The professor draws the parallel with the old gold standard: just as the pound used to be pegged to gold, stablecoins are pegged to the dollar.

**Who uses them:** crypto traders (stablecoins are the 'cash leg' of most Bitcoin trades); **savers in Argentina, Turkey and Nigeria**, who want digital dollars their own banks cannot give them, because their local currencies swing widely and dollars are often unavailable; **migrants** sending money home for cents instead of around 6% in fees (the professor mentions post-office transfer fees of roughly 4–5%); and **firms paying freelancers abroad** — for example a startup paying a freelancer in India.

**Who issues them and why:** **Tether** and **Circle (USDC)** are the two biggest. Their business model is simple and very profitable: you deposit $1,000, you get 1,000 tokens, they pay you **no interest**, and they invest the reserves (for example in government bonds) and keep the interest — billions a year. There is also a government angle: because these issuers buy large amounts of government debt, the **US Treasury gains a new buyer**, which is why Washington **legalised** stablecoins in 2025.

**The catch:** there is **no deposit insurance**. **Terra**, which had no real reserves, went to zero in May 2022. **USDC** fell to **87 cents** when its bank, **SVB**, failed in 2023 — a reminder that the reserves themselves sit in banks and bonds, and both can go wrong. The professor notes stablecoins in circulation had reached about **$420 billion**, and that they are becoming a genuine medium of exchange. *(Slide 21; 1:13:21; 1:19:19)*

**Key points:**

- Stablecoin = crypto token pegged 1:1 to the dollar, with the dollar held in reserve.
- Analogy: like the old peg of currencies to gold, but to the dollar instead.
- Main users: crypto traders, savers in high-inflation countries, remittance senders, firms paying freelancers abroad.
- Tether and Circle (USDC) keep the interest on the reserves and pay holders nothing.
- Governments like them because the issuers are big buyers of government debt; the US legalised them in 2025.
- No deposit insurance: Terra went to zero (May 2022); USDC fell to 87 cents when SVB failed (2023).
- The risk is in the reserves: the bank holding them, or the price of the bonds.

**Examples:**

- A saver in Argentina or Turkey holding digital dollars because local currency is unstable and real dollars are hard to get.
- Sending remittances for cents instead of ~6% fees.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> Place stablecoins on the pyramid: they are the top layer — a *company's* promise, backed by assets that are themselves claims on banks and governments. That is why they can 'break the peg' when a bank fails, and it explains the professor's point that they are closer to money than Bitcoin but less safe than a bank deposit.

## 22. Challenger 3: the digital pound (a CBDC)

A **CBDC** is a **Central Bank Digital Currency**: digital money issued by the central bank itself, that **you** could hold directly. Today only banks hold central-bank money (as reserves); a CBDC would **open the base of the pyramid to everyone**. Around **130 countries (98% of world GDP)** are exploring one; **China's e-CNY is live**; the UK is designing a 'digital pound'.

**Why bother, if nobody is asking for one?** Slide 22 gives three reasons.
1. **A public option.** When the last cash machine closes, every pound you hold is a private bank's promise. A CBDC keeps state money available to the public — real digital cash.
2. **Who controls payments.** The trigger was Facebook's **Libra** in 2019. The ECB wants to reduce dependence on Visa, Mastercard and dollar stablecoins; **China** wanted the state back between Alipay, WeChat and every payment, rather than letting the payment system run through private tech companies.
3. **Cheaper plumbing.** Card fees cost UK shops over **£1bn a year**; a CBDC could be near-free, instant, and able to work **offline** when networks fail.

**The perils** are privacy and bank stability. On privacy, the state could in principle see every payment — politically very sensitive. On banks, deposits could drain into the CBDC, which would make a bank run almost instant. That is why the design **caps holdings at £10,000 to £20,000 and pays no interest**: it is meant as a backstop, not a savings account.

The professor pushes this further in discussion. If there were no cap and no interest limit, everyone could move their savings into a central-bank account overnight — and then you would have to **rethink the whole architecture of the financial system**, because banks depend on deposits to lend, and interest rates and inflation policy would work in an entirely different way. His view is that the cap is a sensible negotiated compromise with the banks: a wallet-sized amount that also usefully converts idle cash lying around into digital state money. *(Slide 22; Slide 24; 1:19:19; 1:25:01)*

**Key points:**

- CBDC = digital central-bank money that the public could hold directly.
- It opens the safest layer of the pyramid to everyone, not just banks.
- ~130 countries exploring; China's e-CNY live; UK designing a digital pound.
- Motivations: a public alternative to private bank money; control of the payment system; cheaper, instant, offline-capable payments.
- Risks: state visibility over payments (privacy), and faster, easier bank runs.
- Design response: holding caps of ~£10,000–£20,000 and no interest — a backstop, not savings.
- Without caps, the whole structure of banking and monetary policy would need redesigning.

**Examples:**

- Facebook's Libra (2019) as the event that pushed central banks to act.
- China wanting the state back in the middle of payments dominated by Alipay and WeChat.
- UK card fees of over £1bn a year for shops.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> Notice the pattern across all three challengers: each one is really a question about *whose promise you are holding*. Bitcoin answers 'nobody's', stablecoins answer 'a company's', and a CBDC answers 'the state's'. That is probably why Slide 23 asks you to place each contender on the pyramid.

## How the ideas connect

- Trust is the thread of the whole lecture: it explains why a £10 note has value (Slide 2), why fiat money works (Slide 8), why hyperinflation happens when trust dies (Slide 10), why Bitcoin's design replaces trust in a bank with trust in code (Slide 20), and why a CBDC is attractive once cash disappears (Slide 22).
- The three-job test (Slide 5) is used as a tool again and again: on gold, cigarettes, cowrie shells, Bitcoin and stablecoins — so learning it once pays off throughout the lecture.
- The money pyramid (Slide 12) and liquidity (Slide 13) fit together: a bank run is everyone rushing down the pyramid into the smallest layer at the same time, which is why it is a liquidity crisis and not an insolvency crisis.
- Insolvency vs illiquidity, introduced in Lecture 3, is the reason liquidity gets a whole section here: every bank that failed in Lecture 3 was illiquid before it was worthless.
- The liquidity spectrum (Slide 15) explains the liquidity premium, which in turn explains why banks and funds are profitable but fragile: they borrow at the liquid end and lend at the illiquid end.
- Inflation (Slide 9) and hyperinflation (Slide 10) are the same mechanism in different doses — more money chasing the same goods — which is why the cure is institutional (an independent central bank) rather than technical.
- The payment plumbing (Slide 18) links the digital world back to the base of the pyramid: card payments only move deposits, and banks still settle in central-bank reserves — which is exactly the argument for why a CBDC and central-bank money still matter as cash dies.
- Bitcoin, stablecoins and the digital pound are three different answers to one question raised by the pyramid: whose promise should digital money be — nobody's, a company's, or the state's?
- Gold's old role (pegging currency to a commodity) returns in a new form with stablecoins pegging to the dollar, which the professor points out explicitly.
- Liquidity risk connects banks, property funds and pension funds: in 2016, 2020 and 2022 the same gap between promised cash and held assets caused suspensions or forced selling.

## Recap

1. Money is defined by three jobs — medium of exchange, store of value and unit of account — not by what it is made of.
2. Money is not the same as currency: cash is only about 3% of UK money, while about 97% is digital bank deposits, mostly created when banks lend.
3. Modern money is fiat money, backed by nothing but trust, since the US ended gold convertibility in 1971.
4. Inflation is a quiet tax on holding money (BoE target 2%, UK peak 11.1% in October 2022); hyperinflation is the same mechanism with no brake, which is why independent central banks matter.
5. Money forms a pyramid of promises: central-bank money (safest, smallest, ~£745bn) under commercial-bank deposits (~£3.2tn) under e-money — and a bank run is everyone rushing down it at once.
6. Liquidity is how fast and cheaply you can turn an asset into cash without cutting its price; it is not wealth and not solvency, and illiquid assets pay a liquidity premium.
7. Liquidity risk is the gap between what you have promised and what you actually hold, which is why banks, property funds and pension funds all needed central-bank rescues (2020, 2022).
8. Cash is fading (over half of UK payments in 2011 to under 10% today), and the future is a contest between Bitcoin (fails all three money tests), stablecoins (pegged to the dollar, a company's promise) and CBDCs such as a digital pound (state digital cash, capped and interest-free).
