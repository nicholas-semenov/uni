# IC103 L02 — Explanation

> [!info] Пошаговое объяснение (сгенерировано). Лекция: [[IC103 L02 — Elasticity and Consumer Choice]]. Ссылки *(Slide N; MM:SS)* ведут к источнику — сверяйся с ними.

## Overview

This lecture has two main parts. Part 1 explains elasticity — a way of measuring how strongly buyers (and sellers) react when something in the market changes, especially price. You learn the price elasticity of demand (PED) formula, how to read its value, what makes demand elastic or inelastic, how to calculate it (midpoint method and point elasticity), and how elasticity decides whether a price change raises or lowers a firm's total revenue. Part 2 goes 'behind' the demand curve and asks where demand comes from: consumer choice based on utility (satisfaction), willingness to pay, consumer surplus, and then indifference curves, budget lines and the income and substitution effects. The point is that demand is not just given — it comes from consumers trying to get the most satisfaction from a limited budget.

## 1. Quick recap of Lecture 1: finding equilibrium

Before starting the new topic, the lecturer finished a practice exercise from Lecture 1. The demand function was Qd = 50 − 5P and the supply function was Qs = 10 + 3P. To find equilibrium you set quantity demanded equal to quantity supplied: 50 − 5P = 10 + 3P. Moving the P terms to one side gives 40 = 8P, so P = 5.

To find the equilibrium quantity you put P = 5 back into either equation — it does not matter which, because at equilibrium both give the same answer. Using demand: 50 − 5(5) = 25. Using supply: 10 + 3(5) = 25. So equilibrium price = 5 and equilibrium quantity = 25. *(00:00)*

**Key points:**

- Equilibrium is found by setting Qd = Qs and solving for P.
- Substitute the equilibrium price back into demand OR supply — both give the same quantity.
- Answer in the example: P = 5, Q = 25.

**Examples:**

- Qd = 50 − 5P, Qs = 10 + 3P → P = 5, Q = 25.

## 2. What 'elasticity' means

In Lecture 1 you learned that price affects quantity demanded and quantity supplied. Lecture 2 takes one step further and asks: *by how much?* That is what elasticity measures.

The definition on the slide is: "Elasticity measures how much buyers and sellers respond to changes in market conditions." The two examples of 'market conditions' given are price and income. If we talk only about demand, elasticity tells us how much buyers respond. Price elasticity of demand is defined as "the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price". *(Slide 4; 06:02)*

**Key points:**

- Elasticity = responsiveness to a change in market conditions.
- The main 'conditions' mentioned in the lecture are price and income.
- Price elasticity of demand (PED) = how sensitive quantity demanded is to a price change.

**Examples:**

- Price change and income change are the two examples of market conditions named on Slide 4.

## 3. The PED formula and why the sign is normally negative

The formula is:

PεD = (% change in quantity demanded) ÷ (% change in price)

In the slide notation, εD means elasticity of demand, PεD means price elasticity of demand, %∆QD is the percentage change in quantity demanded and %∆P is the percentage change in price.

The number is normally negative, because the demand curve slopes downwards: price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions. The lecturer showed both directions: if price rises from £4 to £5, quantity demanded falls (for example from 100 to 90) — a positive denominator with a negative numerator gives a negative answer. If price falls from £6 to £5, quantity demanded rises (for example 80 to 100) — a negative denominator with a positive numerator again gives a negative answer. Because the sign is always the same, in practice we usually talk about the **absolute value** (we ignore the minus sign). *(Slide 5; 06:02; 12:09)*

**Key points:**

- PεD = %∆QD / %∆P.
- The result is normally negative because of the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded (law of demand).
- In practice we compare the absolute value of elasticity with 1.

**Examples:**

- Price £4 → £5, quantity 100 → 90 (negative over positive = negative elasticity).
- Price £6 → £5, quantity 80 → 100 (positive over negative = negative elasticity).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> A practical exam tip: write the minus sign if you calculate it, but when you classify demand as elastic or inelastic, compare |PεD| with 1. This is exactly what the slide means by 'use the absolute value'.

## 4. Reading the number: elastic, unit elastic, inelastic

Using absolute values:

- **PεD > 1 → elastic demand.** The percentage change in quantity demanded is proportionally *larger* than the percentage change in price (numerator bigger than denominator).
- **PεD = 1 → unit elastic demand.** The two percentage changes are exactly the same size.
- **PεD < 1 → inelastic demand.** The percentage change in quantity demanded is proportionally *smaller* than the percentage change in price (numerator smaller than denominator).

The class poll checked this: if price rises by 10% and quantity demanded falls by 20%, then |PεD| = 20/10 = 2, which is greater than 1, so demand is **elastic**. *(Slide 5; Slide 6; 12:09)*

**Key points:**

- Compare the size of the two percentage changes, not the absolute numbers of units.
- >1 elastic, =1 unit elastic, <1 inelastic.
- Elastic means buyers react strongly; inelastic means they react weakly.

**Examples:**

- Menti question: price +10%, quantity −20% → elasticity 2 → elastic demand (Slide 6).

## 5. What makes demand more or less elastic

Two determinants were covered.

**1. Availability of close substitutes.** Goods with close substitutes are usually more elastic. If the price rises, consumers can easily switch to the substitute. The lecturer's example: if your favourite brand of coffee becomes more expensive, you can simply buy a different brand.

**2. Necessities versus luxuries.** Necessities are usually more inelastic — people keep buying them even if the price rises. Luxuries are usually more elastic — you can postpone or avoid the purchase. The slide's example: if the price of food rises, quantity demanded may not change very much; if the price of a luxury item rises, quantity demanded may fall much more.

The general rule on the slide: "The easier it is to avoid or replace a good, the more elastic demand tends to be." The poll applied this: of prescription medicine, electricity, a particular brand of coffee, and tap water, the most price-elastic is **a particular brand of coffee**, because it is the easiest one to replace with something else. *(Slide 7; Slide 8; 12:09; 18:08)*

**Key points:**

- More close substitutes → more elastic demand.
- Necessities → more inelastic; luxuries → more elastic.
- Core rule: the easier the good is to avoid or replace, the more elastic demand is.

**Examples:**

- Switching to another coffee brand when your usual brand gets more expensive.
- Food (necessity) versus a luxury item.
- Poll: brand of coffee is more elastic than prescription medicine, electricity or tap water.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> Notice the difference between a *market* and a *brand*: demand for coffee in general is less elastic than demand for one particular brand of coffee, because the brand has close substitutes while the product category does not. The lecture's poll implies this, but does not state it as a separate rule.

## 6. The five types of demand elasticity and their graphs

Slides 9 and 10 (images taken from Mankiw) show five cases. The lecturer went through them one by one and said clearly: **do not memorise the graphs**; understand how the percentage changes compare.

1. **Perfectly inelastic (elasticity = 0).** The demand curve is vertical. Raising the price (example: $4 → $5) leaves quantity demanded completely unchanged, so %∆Q = 0. Example given: life-saving medicine — there is no substitute, so you buy it at any price.
2. **Inelastic (elasticity < 1).** Price rises from $4 to $5 (about a 22% rise in the lecturer's calculation) and quantity falls from 100 to 90 (about 11%) → 11/22 < 1. Example: petrol — hard to avoid using it.
3. **Unit elastic (elasticity = 1).** Price rises by about 22% and quantity falls by about 22% → 22/22 = 1. The lecturer called this mainly a theoretical case.
4. **Elastic (elasticity > 1).** Price rises about 22% but quantity falls about 67% → greater than 1. Example: holidays — if your preferred destination becomes too expensive, you can easily choose a similar place instead.
5. **Perfectly elastic (elasticity = infinity).** At exactly $4 consumers will buy any quantity, but if the price rises even slightly, buyers stop buying completely and switch to other sellers. Also described as mainly theoretical. *(Slide 9; Slide 10; 18:08; 23:53)*

**Key points:**

- Five cases: perfectly inelastic (0), inelastic (<1), unit elastic (=1), elastic (>1), perfectly elastic (∞).
- Perfectly inelastic = vertical demand curve; perfectly elastic = horizontal demand curve.
- Real-world examples: life-saving medicine (perfectly inelastic), petrol (inelastic), holidays (elastic).
- Unit elastic and perfectly elastic were described as theoretical cases.
- The lecturer does not require memorising the diagrams, only understanding the percentage comparison.

**Examples:**

- Life-saving medicine: price can change, quantity demanded does not.
- Petrol: price rises a lot, quantity demanded falls only a little.
- Holidays: price rises, you change destination, so quantity falls a lot.

## 7. Measuring elasticity between two points: the midpoint (arc) method

Slide 11 lists three ways to measure elasticity: the simple percentage method between two points, the arc (midpoint) method between two points, and point elasticity at a single point.

The simple percentage method uses the usual idea of percentage change: (new value − old value) ÷ old value. So PεD = [(Q2 − Q1)/Q1] ÷ [(P2 − P1)/P1]. The problem is that the answer depends on which point you start from. With point A (P = £4, Q = 120) and point B (P = £6, Q = 80):

- A → B: (80−120)/120 ÷ (6−4)/4 = −0.66
- B → A: (120−80)/80 ÷ (4−6)/6 = −1.5

These are the same two points, so ideally we want one single answer. The **solution is the midpoint method**: divide the change by the *average* of the two values instead of the starting value:

PεD = [(Q2 − Q1) ÷ ((Q2+Q1)/2)] ÷ [(P2 − P1) ÷ ((P2+P1)/2)]

For the same example: (80−120)/100 ÷ (6−4)/5 = −0.4/0.4 = **−1**, and you get the same answer going from B to A. The lecturer stressed that this is different from the percentage change you have probably used before, so it needs practice. *(Slide 11; Slide 12; 29:46; 36:05)*

**Key points:**

- Elasticity usually differs along a demand curve, so we talk about elasticity *between two points* or *at a point*, not of the whole curve.
- The simple percentage method gives different answers depending on the direction of movement.
- The midpoint (arc) method divides by the average of the two quantities and the average of the two prices.
- The midpoint method gives the same elasticity in both directions.

**Examples:**

- A (£4, 120) and B (£6, 80): simple method gives −0.66 or −1.5; midpoint method gives −1 both ways.

## 8. Measuring elasticity at a single point: point elasticity

Start from PεD = %∆QD/%∆P = (∆QD/QD) ÷ (∆P/P) = (∆QD/∆P) × (P/Q). At a single point, the change becomes infinitely small, so the ratio becomes a derivative:

PεD = (dQD/dP) × (P/Q)

Here dQD/dP shows how sensitive quantity demanded is to a very small price change around that point — it is the first derivative of the demand function QD = f(P). On a graph, price is on the Y axis and quantity on the X axis, so what you see is the *inverse* demand function P = f(QD); its slope dP/dQD is found by drawing a tangent to the curve at that point. Because of this, the slide gives the practical version:

PεD = (1 / slope) × (P/Q)

The lecturer said she would not go deep into the technical side; what you must remember is this formula and that the slope comes from the tangent. *(Slide 11; Slide 13; 36:05)*

**Key points:**

- Point elasticity uses a derivative because the change at one point is infinitesimally small.
- PεD = (dQD/dP) × (P/Q).
- Using the drawn demand curve: PεD = (1/slope) × (P/Q), where the slope is the slope of the tangent at that point.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The '1/slope' version appears because the graph puts price on the vertical axis: the slope you can read off the diagram is dP/dQ, and elasticity needs dQ/dP, which is its reciprocal. The slides state the formula but do not explain this reason explicitly.

## 9. Point elasticity example, and why elasticity changes along the same curve

The lecturer worked through a graphical example at a point (called R) where price = 30 and quantity = 40. Drawing a tangent to the demand curve at that point gives a slope of 50/100 in absolute value (from 0 − 50 over 100 − 0). So:

PεD = (100/50) × (30/40) = 1.5

Because this is greater than 1, demand at that point is **elastic** (Slide 14).

This leads to an important result that students often find surprising (Slide 15): on a **straight-line** demand curve the slope is constant, but elasticity is **not** constant. The reason is in the formula: the slope part stays the same, but the P/Q part changes as you move along the line. The lecturer read off different points with different price/quantity combinations, so the fractions P/Q differ and the elasticity differs. *(Slide 14; Slide 15; 36:05; 41:56)*

**Key points:**

- Worked example: slope 50/100, P = 30, Q = 40 → PεD = 1.5 → elastic at that point.
- Elasticity is not the same at every point on a demand curve.
- Even on a straight line (constant slope), elasticity varies because P/Q varies.
- At different points you get a different tangent (for curves) and different P and Q values.

**Examples:**

- Point R: (1/slope) × (P/Q) = (100/50) × (30/40) = 1.5.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> A helpful way to remember it: high up on a straight-line demand curve price is high and quantity is low, so P/Q is large and demand is elastic; low down, P/Q is small and demand is inelastic. The lecture states that elasticity varies but does not spell out this high/low pattern.

## 10. Why elasticity matters: firms and policymakers

**For a firm (pricing strategy).** A price cut only makes sense if the increase in sales is big enough to make up for receiving less money per unit. If you are a business owner and you cut your price, the law of demand says quantity demanded will rise — but you need to know *by how much*. Elasticity answers exactly that question: whether the extra sales will cover the loss from charging less per unit.

**For a government (public policy).** If demand for a product is very inelastic, raising its price through taxation may not reduce consumption very much. The slide's example is cigarettes: price up 10% → quantity demanded down only 2%. The lecturer explained the chain: the tax raises the firm's costs, so the price per cigarette rises, so quantity demanded falls — but possibly by a much smaller percentage than the price rise, which means the policy aim of reducing smoking is only partly achieved.

This is also where the lecture introduces the measure used in the next part: total consumer expenditure (TE) or total revenue (TR) = P × Q. *(Slide 16; 41:56)*

**Key points:**

- Elasticity tells a firm whether a price cut will raise or lower revenue.
- If demand is inelastic, a tax that raises price reduces consumption only a little.
- Slide example: cigarettes, price +10% → quantity −2% (inelastic).
- TE = TR = P × Q links elasticity to money.

**Examples:**

- Cigarette taxation: price rises 10%, quantity demanded falls only 2%.
- A business deciding whether cutting its price per unit will pay off.

## 11. Total consumer expenditure = total revenue = P × Q

Total consumer expenditure (TE) is the amount consumers spend on a good: the price per unit multiplied by the number of units bought, so TE = P × Q.

The same number can be read from the other side of the market. What consumers spend is exactly what firms receive, so total revenue (TR) = price per unit × number of units sold. In this simple setting: **TE = TR = P × Q**. The only difference is the point of view: the consumer's view (spending) or the firm's view (revenue).

On a diagram with price on the Y axis and quantity on the X axis, TE is the **area of the rectangle** formed by the price and the quantity purchased. Slide example: if P = £2 and Q = 3 million, then TE = TR = £6 million. *(Slide 17; 47:47)*

**Key points:**

- TE = P × Q, from the consumer's point of view.
- TR = P × Q, from the firm's point of view.
- In this simple model the two are equal.
- Graphically it is the rectangle under the price line up to the quantity bought.

**Examples:**

- P = £2, Q = 3 million → TE = TR = £6 million.

## 12. How a price change affects total revenue: case by case

This is the practical pay-off of elasticity. The logic is always the same: P × Q, where one of them moves up and the other moves down, so what matters is which percentage change is bigger.

- **Elastic demand (Slide 18).** Price falls from £5 to £4, so the firm gets less per unit. But because demand is elastic, quantity rises by a *larger* proportion than the price fell (in the diagram the lecturer read quantity moving from 10 to 20). The extra sales more than compensate, so **TE and TR rise**.
- **Inelastic demand (Slide 19).** Price rises. Consumers buy less, but only by a relatively small percentage. The firm receives more per unit and loses few sales, so **TR rises**.
- **Unit elastic demand (Slide 20).** The middle case: the percentage change in quantity exactly matches the percentage change in price, so the two effects cancel and **TE/TR stay the same**.
- **Perfectly inelastic demand (Slide 21).** The demand curve is vertical; quantity does not change at all. If price rises from P1 to P2, the same quantity is bought at a higher price, so **TE rises** simply because of the price.
- **Perfectly elastic demand (Slide 22).** The demand curve is horizontal. At the market price consumers buy the quantity offered, but if the price rises even slightly, quantity demanded falls to zero and buyers switch to other sellers.

Slide 23 summarises all of this with arrows. The lecturer's way of reading it: with elastic demand, if P rises (one arrow up) Q falls proportionally more (two arrows down), so total revenue falls; if P falls, Q rises proportionally more, so total revenue rises. With inelastic demand, if P rises, Q falls proportionally less, so total revenue rises. The poll on Slide 24 confirms it: if demand is elastic and a firm lowers its price, total revenue will most likely **increase**. *(Slides 18-24; 47:47; 53:53; 59:43)*

**Key points:**

- Elastic demand: price down → revenue up; price up → revenue down.
- Inelastic demand: price up → revenue up; price down → revenue down.
- Unit elastic: revenue unchanged when price changes.
- Perfectly inelastic: revenue moves in the same direction as price, because quantity never changes.
- Perfectly elastic: a tiny price rise above the market price sends quantity demanded to zero.
- You can remember the summary either from the diagrams or from the arrow table on Slide 23.

**Examples:**

- Elastic case on Slide 18: price £5 → £4, quantity 10 → 20 in the lecturer's reading of the diagram.
- Menti (Slide 24): elastic demand + price cut → total revenue increases.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> A compact rule of thumb (my own summary, consistent with Slide 23): revenue moves in the same direction as *quantity* when demand is elastic, and in the same direction as *price* when demand is inelastic.

## 13. Other demand elasticities: income and cross-price

The logic of elasticity can be reused with a different denominator.

**Income elasticity of demand = %∆QD ÷ %∆Y** (Y = income). It asks how much quantity demanded changes when income changes. The slide gives three cases:
- Normal goods: positive income elasticity (income rises → quantity demanded rises).
- Inferior goods: negative income elasticity (income rises → quantity demanded falls).
- Necessities: positive but **small** income elasticity (income rises → quantity demanded rises only slightly).

**Cross-price elasticity of demand, CεDAB = %∆QD of A ÷ %∆P of B.** It measures how the quantity demanded of one good responds to the price of a *related* good:
- Substitutes: positive cross-price elasticity. If the price of B rises, people buy more A instead.
- Complements: negative cross-price elasticity. If the price of B rises, quantity demanded of A falls. The lecturer explained the chain: the price of B rises → less B is bought → because A is used together with B, less A is bought too. *(Slide 26; 59:43)*

**Key points:**

- Income elasticity: %∆QD / %∆income.
- Positive for normal goods, negative for inferior goods, positive but small for necessities.
- Cross-price elasticity: %∆QD of A / %∆P of B.
- Positive for substitutes, negative for complements.
- The sign of these elasticities is informative (unlike PED, where the sign is almost always negative).

**Examples:**

- Substitutes: price of B rises → buy more A instead (positive cross-price elasticity).
- Complements: price of B rises → quantity demanded of A also falls (negative).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The lecture does not give named goods here. Common textbook examples (my background knowledge, not from these sources) are coffee and tea as substitutes, and coffee and milk/sugar as complements — the coffee-and-milk pairing does appear later on Slide 40 in a different context.

## 14. Price elasticity of supply

The same idea applied to the seller's side: PεS = %∆QS ÷ %∆P. It measures how responsive quantity supplied is to a price change.

The **sign is positive**, because there is a positive relationship between price and quantity supplied. Classification works the same way: PεS > 1 elastic supply (quantity supplied changes by a larger percentage than price); PεS < 1 inelastic supply (smaller percentage); PεS = 1 unit elastic supply (same percentage).

The determinant you must remember is **time**: supply is more elastic in the long run, because producers have more time to adjust production. The lecturer's example: if the price of strawberries rises, you can sell more today but only a little, because you must plant new strawberries, wait for them to grow, and only then sell them. Measurement uses the same two tools as for demand: the arc/midpoint method and point elasticity (dQS/dP × P/Q). *(Slide 27; 1:05:52)*

**Key points:**

- PεS = %∆QS / %∆P and is positive.
- >1 elastic, <1 inelastic, =1 unit elastic supply.
- Key determinant: time — supply is more elastic in the long run.
- Same measurement methods: midpoint (arc) and point elasticity.

**Examples:**

- Strawberries: after a price rise, a producer cannot immediately sell much more because the crop must be planted and grown.

## 15. Consumer choice and utility: where demand comes from

Until now the demand curve was simply given to us. Now the lecture asks where it comes from. Imagine you have £10: you cannot buy everything you want, so you must choose. The choices of many consumers together explain the market demand we observe. The lecture says consumer choice depends on three things: **utility** (satisfaction), **income or budget**, and the **prices** of goods and services.

**Utility** is the satisfaction a consumer gets from consuming a good or service. **Utility theory** assumes rational consumers make choices in order to maximise their utility. Two measures are used:
- **Total utility (TU)**: the total satisfaction from all the units of a good consumed in a given time period, TU = U(Q).
- **Marginal utility (MU)**: the additional satisfaction from consuming one *extra* unit in a given period, MU = dTU/dQ.

Remember from the earlier material: 'marginal' always means one extra unit, or one small change. *(Slide 29; Slide 30; 1:05:52)*

**Key points:**

- Consumer choice explains demand.
- Three factors: utility, income/budget, prices.
- Utility = satisfaction from consuming a good or service.
- Rational consumers are assumed to maximise utility.
- TU = total satisfaction from all units; MU = extra satisfaction from one more unit.

**Examples:**

- How to spend £10 (Slide 29).

## 16. Diminishing marginal utility

The principle of diminishing marginal utility says: the more of a product a person consumes, the less additional utility they gain from one more unit. In simple words, the more units of the same product you consume, the less extra satisfaction you normally get from the next one.

The class poll used pizza: if you are very hungry and eat slices of pizza, the slice giving the greatest *additional* satisfaction is the **first slice**. Slide 33 shows the same idea with crisps, and adds an important extra point: if you have already eaten far too much, consuming another unit may actually *reduce* your satisfaction — that is, marginal utility can become negative. *(Slide 31; Slide 32; Slide 33; 1:12:08)*

**Key points:**

- Each extra unit normally adds less satisfaction than the one before.
- This is about *marginal* utility falling, while total utility can still be rising.
- After a certain point, an extra unit can even reduce satisfaction (negative MU).

**Examples:**

- Pizza slices: the first slice gives the biggest extra satisfaction (Slide 32).
- Crisps: eating far too much means another unit lowers satisfaction (Slide 33).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> Diminishing marginal utility is the reason why demand curves slope downwards: because each extra unit is worth less to you, you are only willing to buy more if the price is lower. The lecture implies this link in the consumer-surplus diagrams but does not say it as a single sentence.

## 17. Willingness to pay and consumer surplus

Utility is abstract — we cannot see 'satisfaction' or measure it in natural units. So economists use money as a measure. **Willingness to pay (WTP)** is the maximum amount a consumer is prepared to pay for a good or service; in other words, how much the satisfaction is worth to them. So WTP = the monetary value of utility.

**Consumer surplus** is the difference between what a consumer is willing to pay and what they actually pay. The lecturer's example: if her WTP for a coffee is £5 and she pays £3, her consumer surplus is £2 — that is her gain.

On a diagram, the demand curve can be read as the willingness to pay for each extra unit. The area below the demand curve and above the market price is the consumer surplus (shown as a triangle on Slide 36), while the rectangle below the price is total expenditure/firm revenue. The lecturer read numbers from the diagram: at the first unit WTP is about £45 while the price is £30; at the second unit WTP is £40 while the price is £30, giving £10 of surplus on that unit; and so on. *(Slide 34; Slide 36; 1:18:06)*

**Key points:**

- WTP = maximum amount a consumer will pay = monetary value of utility.
- Consumer surplus = WTP − price actually paid.
- The demand curve can be interpreted as a willingness-to-pay curve.
- On the graph: area under the demand curve above the price = consumer surplus; area under the price = total expenditure.

**Examples:**

- Coffee: WTP £5, price paid £3 → consumer surplus £2.
- Diagram reading: WTP £40 on a unit, price £30 → £10 surplus on that unit.

## 18. Optimal consumption with one good: Q1, Q2 and Q3

Two more definitions first. **Marginal consumer surplus (MCS)** is the difference between what you are willing to pay for one more unit and what you are actually charged: MCS = MU − P, where MU is marginal utility expressed in money. **Total consumer surplus (TCS)** is the sum of the marginal surpluses of all units consumed: TCS = TU − TE. Rational consumer behaviour is defined as the attempt to **maximise total consumer surplus**.

Now the three diagram steps:
- **At Q1 (Slide 37), price P1.** The demand curve shows the WTP for the next unit. At Q1, WTP = MU = P1, so the final unit is worth exactly what it costs and its marginal consumer surplus is zero. All earlier units had WTP > P1, so they gave positive surplus. That is why Q1 is the optimal quantity at price P1.
- **Price falls to P2 (Slide 38).** Buying only Q1 is no longer optimal, because at Q1 the consumer's WTP is still above the new, lower price — so there is an incentive to buy more. As consumption rises, marginal utility (WTP) falls, until at Q2 we again have WTP = MU = P2. So Q2 is the new optimal quantity.
- **Why Q3 is too much (Slide 39).** Suppose the price stays at P2 but the consumer buys Q3 > Q2. At Q3 marginal utility has fallen to P3, but the consumer still pays P2. So the extra satisfaction from the last unit is worth less than its price: MU < P, and marginal consumer surplus is negative. Q3 is therefore too much consumption at price P2.

The general rule: the consumer keeps buying while MU > P and stops where **MU = P**. *(Slide 35; Slide 37; Slide 38; Slide 39; 1:18:06; 1:23:58)*

**Key points:**

- MCS = MU − P; TCS = TU − TE.
- Rational consumers try to maximise total consumer surplus.
- Optimal quantity is where WTP = MU = price.
- If price falls, the optimal quantity rises (this is the link to the demand curve).
- Consuming beyond the optimum gives negative marginal consumer surplus.

**Examples:**

- Q1 at P1: last unit's surplus = 0, earlier units' surplus > 0.
- Price falls P1 → P2: consumer expands consumption to Q2.
- Q3 at price P2: MU = P3 < P2, so the last unit is not worth its price.

## 19. From one good to a choice between goods: the equi-marginal principle

The one-good model is limited for two reasons given on Slide 40. First, the value of spending an extra £1 can change: it depends on how much income is left and what else you could buy with that £1. Second, the utility you get from one good may depend on other goods — for example, coffee may give more satisfaction when consumed with milk or sugar.

So we need a rule for choosing a *combination* of goods. The **equi-marginal principle**: consumers maximise total utility by allocating income so that the **marginal utility per £1 spent is equal across goods**. If one good gives more extra utility per £1, you should spend more on that good until the ratios are equal.

The lecture ended here; the lecturer said the next session would start from Slide 41 (indifference analysis). *(Slide 40; 1:23:58)*

**Key points:**

- The value of an extra £1 is not constant, and goods can affect each other's utility.
- Equi-marginal principle: MU per £1 spent should be equal for all goods.
- If MU per £1 is higher for one good, buy more of it until the ratios equalise.

**Examples:**

- Coffee gives more satisfaction with milk or sugar (goods are related).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> In formula form this is usually written MUx/Px = MUy/Py. The slides state the principle in words only, so the formula is my own addition.

## 20. Indifference curves and the marginal rate of substitution (slides only)

Indifference analysis is an alternative approach: it does **not** measure utility in numerical units. Instead it compares different combinations ('bundles') of two goods and ranks them according to the consumer's preferences.

An **indifference curve** shows all combinations of two goods that give the consumer the **same** level of satisfaction, so the consumer is indifferent between any two points on the same curve. Slide 43 gives a table for a consumer called Clive, listing combinations of pears and oranges that he likes the same amount as 10 pears and 13 oranges — for example 30 pears and 6 oranges, 24 pears and 7 oranges, 20 pears and 8 oranges, 14 pears and 10 oranges, 8 pears and 15 oranges.

The **marginal rate of substitution (MRS)** is the slope of the indifference curve: the rate at which the consumer is willing to give up one good to get more of the other. It usually **diminishes** as you move along the curve. An **indifference map** contains several indifference curves; higher curves mean higher satisfaction, and two indifference curves **cannot intersect** (Slide 47). Slide 46 adds the important warning that consumers cannot simply pick the highest curve they want — income and prices limit what they can actually afford. *(Slides 42-47; 1:23:58; 00:00)*

**Key points:**

- Indifference analysis ranks bundles instead of measuring utility in numbers.
- All points on one indifference curve give equal satisfaction.
- MRS = slope of the indifference curve; it usually diminishes.
- Indifference map: higher curve = higher satisfaction; curves cannot cross.
- Preferences alone do not decide the choice — the budget matters too.

**Examples:**

- Clive's pears and oranges table: 30 & 6, 24 & 7, 20 & 8, 14 & 10, 10 & 13, 8 & 15 all give equal satisfaction (Slide 43).

## 21. The budget line: what the consumer can afford (slides only)

The **budget line** shows all combinations of two goods that a consumer can afford, given their income and the prices of the two goods. It is the boundary of what is affordable (Slide 49).

Two kinds of change are listed:
- A change in **income** causes a **parallel shift** of the budget line (outward if income rises — this was also the answer to the poll on Slide 51).
- A change in the **price of one good** causes the budget line to **rotate**.

Slide 52 explains the rotation clearly for a fall in the price of good X: the consumer can now afford more X than before, but nothing changes about the maximum amount of Y they could buy if they spent all income on Y, because the price of Y has not changed. So only one end of the line moves. *(Slide 48; Slide 49; Slide 50; Slide 51; Slide 52)*

**Key points:**

- Budget line = all affordable combinations of two goods, given income and prices.
- Income change → parallel shift (outward if income rises).
- Price change of one good → rotation of the budget line.
- A fall in Px moves the X-intercept outward but leaves the Y-intercept unchanged.

**Examples:**

- Menti (Slide 51): income rises, prices unchanged → budget line shifts outward in parallel.
- Slide 60 assumptions: income £30, Py = £1, Px falls from £2 to £1.

## 22. Optimal consumer choice: tangency (slides only)

Consumer choice depends on two things: the **budget constraint** (what is affordable) and the consumer's **preferences** (shown by the indifference map).

The consumer chooses the **highest indifference curve that can be reached given the budget constraint**. At an interior optimum, that indifference curve is **tangent** to the budget line — they touch at exactly one point (Slide 54). *(Slide 53; Slide 54; Slide 40)*

**Key points:**

- Choice = preferences + budget constraint together.
- Optimum = highest reachable indifference curve.
- At an interior optimum the indifference curve is tangent to the budget line.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> Tangency means the slope of the indifference curve (MRS) equals the slope of the budget line (the price ratio), which is the graphical version of the equi-marginal principle from Slide 40. The slides state the tangency condition but do not make this link explicitly.

## 23. Income changes: the income–consumption curve, normal and inferior goods (slides only)

The **income–consumption curve** shows how the consumer's chosen combination of goods changes as income changes. Each budget line represents a different income level; for each one the consumer picks a new optimal point, and joining those points gives the curve (Slide 56).

An increase in income shifts the budget lines outward in parallel. Then:
- For **normal goods**, the quantity consumed **increases** (Slide 57 shows a case where both goods behave as normal goods).
- For **inferior goods**, the quantity consumed **decreases**.

Slide 58 explains the word 'inferior' carefully: an inferior good is **not** a bad-quality good. It simply means that as income rises, consumers choose less of it — once people have more income, they may switch from a cheaper alternative towards something they prefer. So as the budget expands, the optimal quantity of the inferior good can actually fall. *(Slide 55; Slide 56; Slide 57; Slide 58; Slide 26)*

**Key points:**

- Income–consumption curve joins the optimal points at different income levels.
- Higher income → parallel outward shift of the budget line.
- Normal good: quantity consumed rises with income.
- Inferior good: quantity consumed falls with income.
- 'Inferior' describes the response to income, not the quality of the good.

**Examples:**

- Slide 57: both goods are normal, so consumption of both rises as income rises.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> This connects directly back to income elasticity of demand on Slide 26: normal goods have positive income elasticity and inferior goods negative. The slides present the two topics separately without stating the link.

## 24. Price changes: the price–consumption curve and deriving the demand curve (slides only)

The **price–consumption curve** shows how the chosen combination of goods changes when the price of one good changes, holding income and other prices constant. An increase in the price of a good rotates the budget line inward, and the quantity consumed of that good falls.

Slide 60 uses these assumptions: income £30, Py = £1, and Px falling from £2 (budget line B1) to £1 (budget line B2); the optimum moves from point j to point k. The slide also notes an interpretation: an **upward-sloping** price–consumption curve suggests X and Y **may be complements**, while a **downward-sloping** one suggests they **may be substitutes**.

Slide 61 shows the key result: each optimal choice gives us a price of good X and the corresponding quantity demanded of X. Plotting all these price–quantity pairs (P1,Q1), (P2,Q2), (P3,Q3), (P4,Q4) gives the **demand curve**. This is the answer to the question asked at the start of Part 2: where does the demand curve come from? Finally, the slide says the total effect of a price change can be divided into a **substitution effect** and an **income effect**. *(Slide 59; Slide 60; Slide 61)*

**Key points:**

- Price–consumption curve: how consumption changes as one price changes, with income and other prices constant.
- A price rise rotates the budget line inward and reduces the quantity consumed.
- Each optimum gives one price–quantity pair; plotting them produces the demand curve.
- Slope of the price–consumption curve hints at whether the goods are complements or substitutes.
- A price change can be split into a substitution effect and an income effect.

**Examples:**

- Slide 60: income £30, Py = £1, Px £2 → £1, optimum moves from j to k.

## 25. Income and substitution effects (slides only)

When good X becomes more expensive, two things happen at the same time (Slide 62).

**1. Substitution effect** — the change caused by **relative prices**. X is now more expensive compared with other goods, so consumers buy less X and switch to alternatives. Example: if coffee becomes more expensive but tea does not, some consumers switch from coffee to tea.

**2. Income effect** — the change caused by **purchasing power**. A price rise reduces the consumer's *real* purchasing power: your income has not changed, but the same money now buys less. Example: you still have £100, but higher prices mean £100 buys fewer goods.

For a **normal good** both effects reduce consumption when the price rises, so quantity demanded clearly falls. Slides 63-64 separate them on a diagram: the total move is from point f to point h (consumption falls from Qx1 to Qx3); the move from f to g isolates the negative substitution effect, and the move from g to h is the negative income effect.

For an **inferior good** (Slides 65-66) the substitution effect is still negative, but the income effect is **positive**: the price rise makes the consumer effectively poorer, and because X is inferior, being poorer encourages *more* consumption of X. So the two effects work in opposite directions, and the positive income effect partially offsets the negative substitution effect. *(Slide 62; Slide 63; Slide 64; Slide 65; Slide 66)*

**Key points:**

- Substitution effect = caused by a change in relative prices; always reduces consumption of the good whose price rose.
- Income effect = caused by a change in real purchasing power.
- Normal good: both effects point the same way, so consumption falls.
- Inferior good: income effect is positive and partly offsets the substitution effect.
- On the diagram, the total effect f → h is split into f → g (substitution) and g → h (income).

**Examples:**

- Coffee becomes more expensive but tea does not → switch to tea (substitution effect).
- You still have £100 but it now buys fewer goods (income effect).

> Not covered in the recording; based on slide text only. The slides say the income effect only *partially* offsets the substitution effect for an inferior good, so quantity demanded still falls. (The extreme case where the income effect wins — a Giffen good — is not mentioned in these materials.)

## How the ideas connect

- The PED formula (%∆Q ÷ %∆P) is the single idea behind everything in Part 1: change the denominator to income and you get income elasticity, to the price of another good and you get cross-price elasticity, and change the numerator to quantity supplied and you get price elasticity of supply.
- Elasticity connects to money through TE = TR = P × Q: whether a price change raises or lowers revenue depends entirely on whether |PεD| is above or below 1.
- The determinants of elasticity (substitutes, necessity versus luxury) explain the real-world examples: life-saving medicine and tap water are hard to replace (inelastic), while a brand of coffee or a holiday destination is easy to replace (elastic).
- Point elasticity, PεD = (1/slope) × (P/Q), explains why elasticity differs along a straight-line demand curve even though the slope is constant: P/Q changes as you move along the line.
- Diminishing marginal utility explains why willingness to pay falls as quantity rises, which is why the demand curve slopes downwards and why consumer surplus appears as the area between the demand curve and the price.
- The one-good rule 'consume until MU = P' is the simple version of the equi-marginal principle (equal MU per £1 across goods), which in indifference analysis becomes the tangency of the indifference curve to the budget line.
- The price–consumption curve closes the loop of the whole lecture: it derives the demand curve that Part 1 simply assumed was given.
- The normal/inferior goods distinction appears twice: as the sign of income elasticity (Slide 26) and as the direction of the income–consumption curve and the income effect (Slides 57-58, 64, 66).

## Recap

1. Price elasticity of demand = %∆quantity demanded ÷ %∆price; it is normally negative, and we compare its absolute value with 1 (>1 elastic, =1 unit elastic, <1 inelastic).
2. Demand is more elastic when close substitutes exist and when the good is a luxury; it is more inelastic for necessities — the easier a good is to avoid or replace, the more elastic its demand.
3. Elasticity between two points should be calculated with the midpoint (arc) method, because dividing by the starting value gives different answers in each direction (−0.66 versus −1.5 instead of −1).
4. At a single point, PεD = (dQ/dP) × (P/Q) = (1/slope) × (P/Q), which is why elasticity varies along a demand curve even when the curve is a straight line with a constant slope.
5. Because TE = TR = P × Q, elasticity decides what a price change does to revenue: with elastic demand a price cut raises revenue, with inelastic demand a price rise raises revenue, and with unit elastic demand revenue is unchanged.
6. The same formula structure gives income elasticity (positive for normal goods, negative for inferior, small for necessities), cross-price elasticity (positive for substitutes, negative for complements) and price elasticity of supply (positive, and more elastic in the long run because time allows producers to adjust).
7. Consumer choice depends on utility, income and prices; marginal utility diminishes as you consume more, and willingness to pay is the money value of utility, so consumer surplus = WTP − price paid, and the optimal quantity is where MU = P.
8. Indifference curves show equally satisfying bundles and budget lines show affordable bundles; the consumer chooses the tangency point, and a price change moves consumption through both a substitution effect (always reducing the dearer good) and an income effect (negative for normal goods, positive for inferior goods).
