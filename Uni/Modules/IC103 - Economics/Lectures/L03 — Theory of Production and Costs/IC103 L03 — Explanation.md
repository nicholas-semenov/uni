## Overview

This lecture moves from the consumer side of economics to the producer (firm) side. It builds the 'background to supply': how a firm turns inputs (labour and capital) into output, how production in the short run is measured (total, average and marginal product), how production costs come from that production process, what changes in the long run (economies of scale), how revenue is measured, and finally how a firm chooses the output that maximises profit. ## 2. The firm's objective: profit = total revenue − total cost

The slides state that the main objective of the firm (the 'theory of the firm') is to **maximise profit**, and that profit is written as:

Profit (TΠ) = Total Revenue (TR) − Total Cost (TC)

In the recording the lecturer repeats this: "the objective of the company, of the firm, is obviously to maximize the profit ... we estimate the profit as the total revenue that company gets ... minus the total cost that company incurs." This single formula explains the structure of the whole lecture: first we study production and costs (the TC side), then revenue (the TR side), then we put them together to find maximum profit. *(Slide 4; 54:42)*

**Key points:**

- Firms are assumed to maximise profit.
- TΠ = TR − TC.
- The lecture is organised around the two parts of this formula: costs first, then revenue.

## 3. Inputs: fixed and variable factors of production

Firms use **inputs** (resources) to produce **output**. The lecturer defines inputs simply as "the resources that are used in the production".

Inputs are of two types. **Fixed inputs** cannot be changed in the short run — examples given in the recording are buildings, machinery and equipment. **Variable inputs** can be changed in the short run — examples given are labour, raw materials, electricity and water.

An important point the lecturer stresses: whether an input is fixed or variable **depends on the time period you consider**, not on the type of input itself. *(Slide 4; 54:42)*

**Key points:**

- Input = resource used in production; output = what is produced.
- Fixed input: cannot be changed in the short run (e.g. buildings, machinery).
- Variable input: can be changed in the short run (e.g. labour, raw materials, electricity, water).
- The same input can be fixed or variable depending on the time period.

**Examples:**

- A bakery can hire more workers in a few weeks, but it cannot make the building bigger in that time, so labour is variable and the building is fixed (57:00 area of the recording).

## 4. Short run and long run

In economics, 'short run' and 'long run' are **not** fixed numbers of weeks, months or years. The lecturer makes this very clear.

**Short run** = a period in which *at least one* input is fixed. The firm can change some inputs but not all of them. **Long run** = a period in which *all* inputs can be changed.

Because of this definition, the length of the short run is different for different firms and different industries. A bakery can expand faster than, for example, a company that needs a new factory. *(Slide 5; Slide 41; 54:42)*

**Key points:**

- Short run: at least one input is fixed.
- Long run: all inputs are variable.
- The definition is about flexibility, not about calendar time.
- The length of the short run differs between firms and industries.

**Examples:**

- Bakery: can hire workers quickly (variable), but cannot expand the building quickly (fixed) → this situation is the short run.

## 5. The production function Q = f(L, K)

The **production function** shows the relationship between the inputs a firm uses and the output it produces. In general form the lecturer writes Q = f(X1, X2, X3, ...), where Q is the quantity of output and the X's are the inputs (fixed and variable).

For simplicity, economists usually reduce this to **two inputs**: labour (L) and capital (K), so Q = f(L, K). Slide 10 adds an important definition: **capital (K) means the physical equipment and facilities used in production** — machinery, tools, buildings — not money. In the recording the lecturer asks the class what capital means and accepts the answers "machinery" and "equipment".

Slide 41 summarises how this works in each time period: in the **short run**, K is fixed and only L changes; in the **long run**, both L and K are variable, which is where economies of scale and scope become relevant. *(Slide 9; Slide 10; Slide 41; 1:00:36; 1:06:51)*

**Key points:**

- Production function = relationship between inputs and output.
- General form: Q = f(X1, X2, ...); simplified form: Q = f(L, K).
- K = physical equipment and facilities, not money.
- Short run: K fixed, L variable. Long run: both variable.

**Examples:**

- Coffee shop: input = one coffee machine (fixed) + number of baristas (variable); output = coffees produced per hour.

## 6. The law of diminishing marginal returns

This is the central idea of short-run production. Slide 6 gives the definition: *when more units of a variable input are added to fixed inputs, there comes a point when each additional unit of the variable input produces less extra output than the previous one.*

The simple logic: the fixed input (machine, oven, space) eventually becomes a **bottleneck**. In the recording, a student answers that it happens because "it's only one coffee machine", and the lecturer agrees: many workers have to share the same machine and the same counter space, so they wait, and each extra worker adds less and less.

Two warnings the lecturer repeats. First, diminishing returns is about the **extra** output falling, not about total output falling. Total output can still be rising. Second, 'eventually' matters: at the beginning, extra workers may add *more* output than the previous one. *(Slide 6; Slide 7; Slide 8; 1:00:36)*

**Key points:**

- Also called diminishing marginal productivity or simply diminishing returns.
- It only happens because at least one input is fixed → it is a short-run idea.
- The fixed input becomes a bottleneck.
- Falling MPP does not mean falling total output (at first).

**Examples:**

- Coffee shop with one machine: 1st barista → output 20; 2nd → total 45, so extra = 25; then the extra output of further baristas falls to 17, then 8, then 3 (around 1:00:36).
- Menti question (Slide 8): 'A coffee shop has one coffee machine. As more baristas are added, what does diminishing marginal returns mean?' Correct answer: 'Each additional barista eventually adds less extra output.'

## 7. Three measures of short-run production: TP, AP and MP

To analyse short-run production we use three measures. Slide 12 gives the easy version and Slide 14 gives the formal version (the names with 'physical' mean exactly the same thing):

- **Total Product (TP / TPP)** = total output produced from a given amount of inputs.
- **Average Product (AP / APP)** = output per unit of the variable input. Formula: APP = TPP / Qᵥ. Because labour is the variable input, this is simply **output per worker**: APP = TPP / L.
- **Marginal Product (MP / MPP)** = extra output from one additional unit of the variable input. Formula: MPP = ΔTPP / ΔQᵥ, i.e. **the extra output from one more worker**.

The lecturer says the formal definitions sound complicated but the ideas are simple, and recommends remembering them as: total output / output per worker / extra output from one more worker. *(Slide 12; Slide 13; Slide 14; 1:06:51)*

**Key points:**

- TPP = total output.
- APP = TPP ÷ number of workers = output per worker.
- MPP = change in TPP ÷ change in number of workers = extra output from one more worker.
- 'Physical product' simply means output measured in units of goods, not in money.

**Examples:**

- Menti question (Slide 13): 'Which measure tells us the extra output produced by one additional worker?' Correct answer: Marginal Product.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The word 'physical' is useful later: physical product is measured in tonnes, coffees, loaves etc., while cost and revenue are measured in pounds. This is why MPP and MC are different but related measures.

## 8. The farm example: calculating TPP, APP and MPP

Slide 15 shows a farm where capital (K) is fixed and labour (L) is the variable input. The numbers are:

| Workers | TPP | APP = TPP/L | MPP = ΔTPP/ΔL |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | – | – |
| 1 | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| 2 | 10 | 5 | 7 |
| 3 | 24 | 8 | 14 |
| 4 | 36 | 9 | 12 |
| 5 | 40 | 8 | 4 |
| 6 | 42 | 7 | 2 |
| 7 | 42 | 6 | 0 |
| 8 | 40 | 5 | −2 |

How to read it: with 2 workers, APP = 10 ÷ 2 = 5. The MPP of the 2nd worker = 10 − 3 = 7; of the 3rd = 24 − 10 = 14; of the 4th = 36 − 24 = 12.

Slide 15 states the conclusion: **diminishing marginal returns begin when the fourth worker is added, because MPP falls from 14 to 12.** Notice that total output is still rising at that moment (24 → 36). *(Slide 15; Slide 16; 1:12:31)*

**Key points:**

- Diminishing returns start where MPP first falls (here, at the 4th worker).
- MPP can be zero (7th worker) and even negative (8th worker).
- When MPP is negative, total output actually falls (42 → 40).

**Examples:**

- Menti question (Slide 16): 'A farm produces 24 tonnes with 3 workers and 36 tonnes with 4 workers. What is the marginal product of the 4th worker?' Answer: 12 tonnes (36 − 24).

## 9. The total product (TPP) curve

Slide 17 plots the same farm numbers: number of farm workers on the horizontal axis, tonnes of wheat per year on the vertical axis. The curve has three stages.

First it is **steep** (up to point b): each extra worker adds a lot of extra output. Then, **after point b**, the curve becomes **flatter**: extra workers still add output, but less and less — this is where diminishing returns set in. Finally at point d the curve reaches its **maximum** (42 tonnes); after that an extra worker adds no extra output, and then total output starts to **fall**.

The key message, repeated several times in the recording and written on Slide 17: *diminishing marginal returns begin before total output starts falling.* *(Slide 17; 1:12:31; 1:18:33)*

**Key points:**

- Steep TPP = high marginal product; flat TPP = low marginal product.
- Point b = where diminishing returns set in.
- Point d = maximum total output.
- After the maximum, extra workers reduce total output.

**Examples:**

- Farm: 42 tonnes is reached with 6 and 7 workers; the 8th worker reduces output to 40 tonnes.

## 10. MPP is the slope of the TPP curve

Slide 18 makes the link between the two graphs. MPP = ΔTPP / ΔL, and ΔY/ΔX is exactly the definition of a **slope**. So the marginal product is the slope of the total product curve.

The worked example on Slide 18: when labour rises by 1 (ΔL = 1) and total product rises by 7 (ΔTPP = 7), MPP = 7/1 = 7. In the recording the lecturer says: "technically, what we've done here, we just took delta Y and divided by delta X. And this is nothing more but just the slope."

Slides 19 and 22 then show how the shape of the two curves matches: as long as MPP is **positive**, TPP keeps rising; when MPP = **zero**, TPP is at its **maximum**; when MPP becomes **negative**, TPP **falls**. Slide 21 marks point b again: up to b, MPP is rising; after b, MPP falls — that is where diminishing returns set in. *(Slide 18; Slide 19; Slide 21; Slide 22; 1:18:33; 1:24:30)*

**Key points:**

- MPP = ΔTPP/ΔL = slope of TPP.
- Steep TPP → high MPP; flat TPP → low MPP.
- MPP > 0 → TPP rising; MPP = 0 → TPP at maximum; MPP < 0 → TPP falling.
- 'TPP is maximised when MPP = 0' (Slide 22).

**Examples:**

- Moving from 1 to 2 workers on the farm: ΔL = 1, ΔTPP = 10 − 3 = 7, so MPP = 7.

## 11. Average product (APP) and its relationship with MPP

Slide 20 adds the APP curve (APP = TPP/L, average output per worker). The rules are:

- If **MPP is above APP**, then **APP rises**.
- If **MPP is below APP**, then **APP falls**.
- Therefore **APP is at its maximum where MPP = APP** (point c on Slide 23).

Both the slide and the lecturer give a memory trick from student life: if your next exam mark is **above** your current average, your average goes **up**; if it is **below** your average, your average goes **down**. The average is pulled towards the newest (marginal) value. *(Slide 20; Slide 23; 1:24:30)*

**Key points:**

- APP = TPP / L.
- MPP > APP → APP rising; MPP < APP → APP falling.
- APP is maximised where the MPP curve crosses the APP curve.
- This is a general maths relationship between any marginal and average value.

**Examples:**

- Exam marks: a new mark above your average raises the average; a new mark below it lowers the average.
- Farm numbers: APP rises 3, 5, 8, 9 while MPP (3, 7, 14, 12) is above it, then APP falls to 8, 7, 6, 5 as MPP (4, 2, 0, −2) drops below it.

## 12. Short-run costs: types of cost and the total cost curves

Slide 26 introduces how costs are measured. Costs are **opportunity costs**, and they split into **explicit costs** (payments to outside suppliers) and **implicit costs** (costs that involve no payment to a third party but involve sacrificing some alternative). Costs are also split into **fixed** and **variable** costs (fixed costs are linked to the idea of sunk costs).

The three total measures are: **Total Fixed Cost (TFC)**, **Total Variable Cost (TVC)** and **Total Cost (TC) = TFC + TVC**.

Slides 27–30 build the picture step by step with a numerical example. TFC stays at £12 at every level of output, so the TFC line is **horizontal**. TVC starts at £0 and grows (0, 10, 16, 21, 28, 40, 60, 91). TC is simply TFC + TVC (12, 22, 28, 33, 40, 52, 72, 103). Slide 30 marks where **diminishing marginal returns set in**: this is where TVC and TC stop getting flatter and start rising more and more steeply. *(Slide 26; Slide 27; Slide 28; Slide 29; Slide 30)*

**Key points:**

- Explicit cost = actual payment; implicit cost = value of a sacrificed alternative.
- TFC does not change with output (horizontal line); TVC rises with output.
- TC = TFC + TVC.
- The TC and TVC curves get steeper once diminishing marginal returns set in.

**Examples:**

- Slide 29 table: at Q = 4, TFC = £12, TVC = £28, so TC = £40; at Q = 7, TFC = £12, TVC = £91, TC = £103.

## 13. Average cost and marginal cost in the short run

Slide 31 defines the per-unit cost measures:

- **AFC = TFC / Q** (total fixed cost per unit)
- **AVC = TVC / Q** (total variable cost per unit)
- **AC = TC / Q = AFC + AVC** (total cost per unit)
- **MC = ΔTC / ΔQ** — the extra cost of producing one more unit. Because fixed costs do not change when output rises, MC = ΔVC/ΔQ, so **all marginal costs are variable costs**.

Slide 32 shows all of these for the same example. Notice AFC falls continuously (12, 6, 4, 3, 2.4, 2, 1.7) because the same £12 is spread over more units. AVC and AC fall first and then rise (AC: 22, 14, 11, 10, 10.4, 12, 14.7), giving the typical **U-shape** drawn on Slide 33. On Slide 33, MC reaches its minimum first (point x), then AVC (point y), then AC (point z). *(Slide 31; Slide 32; Slide 33; Slide 34)*

**Key points:**

- AC = AFC + AVC = TC/Q.
- AFC always falls as output rises (fixed cost spread over more units).
- MC = ΔTC/ΔQ = ΔVC/ΔQ, because fixed cost does not change.
- AC and AVC are U-shaped; MC reaches its minimum before them.

**Examples:**

- Slide 32: moving from Q = 4 (TC = £40) to Q = 5 (TC = £52) gives MC = £12.
- Slide 34 analogy: marginal cost as the extra study time needed to gain one more exam point — the higher your score, the more extra time each additional point costs.

## 14. The link between production and costs: MPP and MC

This is the bridge between Section 1 (production) and Section 2 (costs). Slide 36 states it directly: **diminishing marginal returns occur at the point where MPP reaches its maximum and MC reaches its minimum.**

The reason is in the formulas. MPP = change in output ÷ change in input. MC = change in cost ÷ change in output, and the change in cost comes from the extra input you had to hire. So when each extra worker produces **a lot** of extra output (high MPP), the extra cost per unit of output is **low** (low MC). When MPP falls, MC rises. MPP and MC move in opposite directions.

Slide 39 shows this numerically. Each employee costs £100. The MPP figures are 3, 4, 5, 2, 1, 0.5 units, and the matching MC figures are £100/3 = £33.33, £100/4 = £25, £100/5 = £20, £100/2 = £50, £100/1 = £100, £100/0.5 = £200. When MPP is at its highest (5 units), MC is at its lowest (£20). *(Slide 36; Slide 37; Slide 38; Slide 39)*

**Key points:**

- MPP and MC are mirror images of each other.
- MC is lowest exactly where MPP is highest.
- Diminishing returns (MPP starting to fall) = MC starting to rise.
- With a constant wage, MC = wage ÷ MPP.

**Examples:**

- Slide 39: the 4th employee costs £100 and adds 5 units, so MC = £20 per unit; the 7th employee costs £100 and adds only 0.5 units, so MC = £200 per unit.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)

## 15. The long run: returns to scale, economies of scale and scope

In the long run **all** inputs, and therefore all costs, are variable (Slide 42). The firm can now change its whole **scale of production** and its **production technology**.

Slide 43 describes **returns to scale** — what happens to output when you increase *all* inputs by x%:
- **Increasing returns**: output rises by more than x%.
- **Constant returns**: output rises by exactly x%.
- **Decreasing returns**: output rises by less than x%.

**Economies of scale** (Slide 45): when increasing the scale of production leads to a **lower cost per unit of output**. The slide lists the sources: specialisation of labour, indivisibilities, efficiency of large plant, the 'container principle', multi-stage production, finance and organisation.

**Economies of scope** (Slides 44, 46) are different: when increasing the **range** of products a firm makes reduces the cost of producing each one, mainly by sharing overheads between product lines. Scale = one product, more of it; scope = more different products. *(Slide 42; Slide 43; Slide 44; Slide 45; Slide 46)*

**Key points:**

- Long run: all factors (and all costs) are variable.
- Returns to scale compare the % change in all inputs with the % change in output.
- Economies of scale = lower cost per unit from a bigger scale (one product).
- Economies of scope = lower cost per product from a wider product range (multiple products).

**Examples:**

- The 'container principle' (Slide 45) is one listed source of economies of scale.

## 16. Choosing the best combination of inputs (productive efficiency)

Slide 47 asks a different long-run question: given that you can change every input, what **mix** of inputs minimises cost? Here MPPₐ is the marginal physical product of factor a and Pₐ is the price (cost per unit) of that factor, so **MPP/P is the productivity you get per £ spent**.

The rule for two factors: if MPPₐ/Pₐ > MPPᵦ/Pᵦ, then factor a gives you more output per pound, so costs can be reduced by using **more of a and less of b**. Costs are minimised where MPPₐ/Pₐ = MPPᵦ/Pᵦ.

With many factors the same logic applies: MPPₐ/Pₐ = MPPᵦ/Pᵦ = ... = MPPₙ/Pₙ. This is called the **equi-marginal principle**. *(Slide 47)*

**Key points:**

- MPP/P = extra output per £ spent on that factor.
- Move spending towards the factor with the higher MPP/P.
- Cost is minimised when MPP/P is equal for all factors (equi-marginal principle).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> Not covered in the recording. In practice this is why a firm substitutes machines for workers when wages rise: the wage rise lowers MPP_labour / P_labour, so capital becomes the better value per pound. This example is not in the sources.

## 17. Long-run cost curves: LRAC and LRMC

Slide 50 defines **long-run average cost (LRAC)** as total cost per unit of output in the long run. The typical LRAC curve has three parts: a falling part (**economies of scale**), a flat part (**constant costs**), and a rising part (**diseconomies of scale** — when increasing the scale of production makes the cost per unit **increase**).

Slide 52 adds **long-run marginal cost (LRMC)**, defined as the extra cost of producing one additional unit of output in the long run, in the case of initial economies of scale followed by diseconomies of scale. Slides 51 and 53 are headed 'Alternative long-run average cost curves' and 'Long-run average and marginal cost curves', showing that the LRAC does not always have exactly the same shape.

Slide 49 deliberately repeats the short-run cost diagram (AFC, AVC, AC, MC) just before this, so you can compare short-run and long-run cost behaviour. *(Slide 49; Slide 50; Slide 51; Slide 52; Slide 53)*

**Key points:**

- LRAC = total cost per unit of output in the long run.
- LRAC falls (economies of scale), flattens (constant costs), then rises (diseconomies of scale).
- Diseconomies of scale = cost per unit rises as the firm gets bigger.
- LRMC = extra cost of one more unit in the long run.
- In the long run there is no fixed cost, so there is no AFC curve.

## 18. Revenue: TR, AR, MR — and the price-taker case

Slide 55 defines the revenue side of the profit formula:
- **Total Revenue (TR)** = the total money a firm earns from sales in a period: TR = P × Q.
- **Average Revenue (AR)** = TR / Q. This is the same as **price per unit**.
- **Marginal Revenue (MR)** = ΔTR / ΔQ — the extra revenue from selling one more unit.

Slides 57–59 show the **price-taker** case, which assumes a **perfectly competitive market** (identical products, many sellers). The market price Pₑ is set by market demand and supply, and the individual firm must accept it. Therefore the firm can sell any quantity at that price, so **Pₑ = AR = MR**, and the demand curve facing the single firm is a **horizontal line**, even though the market demand curve slopes downwards.

Slide 60 shows the result: with a constant price of £5, TR = 5 × Q, so the TR curve is a **straight upward-sloping line** (£1,000 at 200 units, £2,000 at 400 units, and so on). *(Slide 55; Slide 57; Slide 58; Slide 59; Slide 60)*

**Key points:**

- TR = P × Q; AR = TR/Q = price; MR = ΔTR/ΔQ.
- Price-taker = perfectly competitive market, firm accepts the market price.
- For a price-taker: P = AR = MR, and the firm's demand curve is horizontal.
- For a price-taker, the TR curve is a straight line through the origin.

**Examples:**

- Slide 60: price fixed at £5; selling 600 units gives TR = £3,000; selling 1,200 units gives TR = £6,000.

## 19. Revenue when the firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve

Most firms are not price-takers: to sell more they must lower the price. Slide 62 gives the example:

| Q | P = AR | TR | MR |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8 | 8 | – |
| 2 | 7 | 14 | 6 |
| 3 | 6 | 18 | 4 |
| 4 | 5 | 20 | 2 |
| 5 | 4 | 20 | 0 |
| 6 | 3 | 18 | −2 |
| 7 | 2 | 14 | −4 |

Here **MR falls faster than AR** and becomes negative. The reason is that to sell one more unit the firm must cut the price on *all* units, not only the extra one.

Slide 63 shows the matching TR curve: it rises, reaches a maximum (£20) and then falls. Slide 65 connects this to elasticity from Lecture 2: where demand is **elastic**, MR is positive and TR rises; where **elasticity = −1**, MR = 0 and TR is at its maximum; where demand is **inelastic**, MR is negative and TR falls. Slide 64 is a reminder from Lecture 2 that elasticity changes along a straight-line demand curve. *(Slide 62; Slide 63; Slide 64; Slide 65)*

**Key points:**

- If the demand curve slopes down, price must fall to sell more, so MR < AR.
- MR can become negative while AR is still positive.
- TR is maximised where MR = 0, which is where price elasticity of demand = −1.
- Elastic range: MR > 0; inelastic range: MR < 0.

**Examples:**

- Slide 62: going from 4 units at £5 (TR = £20) to 5 units at £4 (TR = £20) gives MR = £0 — the gain from the extra unit exactly cancels the price cut on the other units.

## 20. Profit maximisation: total curves and marginal curves

Finally the two sides are put together: **TΠ = TR − TC**. Slide 67 gives the table:

| Q | TR (£) | TC (£) | TΠ (£) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 6 | −6 |
| 1 | 8 | 10 | −2 |
| 2 | 14 | 12 | +2 |
| 3 | 18 | 14 | **+4** |
| 4 | 20 | 18 | +2 |
| 5 | 20 | 25 | −5 |
| 6 | 18 | 36 | −18 |

Using **total curves** (Slides 68–70), maximum profit is where the vertical gap between TR and TC is largest, which is also the top of the TΠ curve — here at Q = 3 with profit £4.

Using **marginal curves** (Slide 71), there are two stages. **Stage 1:** profit is maximised where **MR = MC**; this gives the profit-maximising output Q_M (point e on Slide 73). **Stage 2:** use the **AR** curve to read the price per unit and the **AC** curve to read the cost per unit at Q_M; then profit = (AR × Q_M) − (AC × Q_M), shown as the shaded rectangle on Slide 74. In that diagram AR = £6 and AC = £4⅔, so the profit per unit is about £1⅓ and, at Q = 3, total profit is £4 — the same answer as the total-curve method. *(Slide 67; Slide 68; Slide 69; Slide 70; Slide 71; Slide 72; Slide 73; Slide 74)*

**Key points:**

- TΠ = TR − TC.
- Total method: maximum profit = largest vertical gap between TR and TC.
- Marginal method Stage 1: produce where MR = MC to find Q_M.
- Marginal method Stage 2: profit = (AR − AC) × Q_M, shown as a rectangle.
- Both methods give the same answer.

## How the ideas connect

- Everything in the lecture serves the formula TΠ = TR − TC: Sections 1–3 build the TC side (production → costs), Section 4 builds the TR side, and Section 5 combines them.
- The fixed/variable input distinction defines the short run and long run, and the short run is the only place where diminishing marginal returns can happen (you need a fixed input to create a bottleneck).
- MPP is the slope of the TPP curve, so the shape of TPP (steep → flat → falling) is just a picture of MPP (rising → falling → negative).
- The marginal–average rule appears twice in the same form: MPP pulls APP up or down, and MC pulls AVC and AC up or down; in both cases the average is at its minimum/maximum where the marginal crosses it.
- Diminishing marginal returns in production directly cause rising marginal cost: MPP at its maximum = MC at its minimum (Slide 36), and that is why TVC and TC become steeper (Slide 30).
- Economies of scale (long run) are a different idea from diminishing returns (short run): diminishing returns come from one fixed input, while economies of scale come from changing all inputs together.
- Elasticity of demand from Lecture 2 reappears in revenue: TR is at its maximum exactly where MR = 0 and elasticity = −1.
- The two profit-maximisation methods are the same thing seen differently: the largest gap between TR and TC happens exactly where their slopes are equal, i.e. where MR = MC.

## Recap

1. Firms aim to maximise profit, where profit (TΠ) = total revenue (TR) − total cost (TC).
2. The short run is any period in which at least one input is fixed; in the long run all inputs are variable.
3. The production function Q = f(L, K) links inputs to output, where K means physical equipment and facilities, not money.
4. The law of diminishing marginal returns: adding more of a variable input to a fixed input eventually makes each extra unit add less extra output.
5. TPP is total output, APP = TPP/L is output per worker, and MPP = ΔTPP/ΔL is the extra output of one more worker; MPP is the slope of the TPP curve.
6. TPP is at its maximum when MPP = 0, and APP is at its maximum where MPP = APP.
7. TC = TFC + TVC; AC = AFC + AVC; MC = ΔTC/ΔQ, and MC is at its minimum exactly where MPP is at its maximum.
8. In the long run, economies of scale lower cost per unit and diseconomies raise it, giving the U-shaped LRAC curve.
9. A price-taker has P = AR = MR; a firm with a downward-sloping demand curve has MR below AR, and TR peaks where MR = 0.
10. Profit is maximised where MR = MC, and the amount of profit is then measured as (AR − AC) × Q.
