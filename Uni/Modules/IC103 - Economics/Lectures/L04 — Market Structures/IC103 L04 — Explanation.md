# IC103 L04 — Explanation

> [!info] Пошаговое объяснение (сгенерировано). Лекция: [[IC103 L04 — Market Structures]]. Ссылки *(Slide N; MM:SS)* ведут к источнику — сверяйся с ними.

## Overview

This lecture (Lecture 4 of IC103) is about market structures — that is, how competitive a market is and how this changes the way firms behave. It compares four structures: perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition and oligopoly, looking at the number of firms, barriers to entry, whether products are identical or differentiated, and the shape of the demand curve each firm faces. For each structure the lecture uses the same profit-maximisation rule (MR = MC) and then shows what happens in the short run and the long run. This matters because market structure affects prices, output, profits, consumer choice and government regulation (Slide 4; 06:07).

## 1. Why market structure matters

A "market structure" describes how a market is organised: how many firms sell in it, how easy it is to enter, and what kind of products are sold. The slide gives one short reason why it matters: **market structure affects how firms behave** (Slide 4).

In the recording the lecturer expands this. Market structure affects how firms decide prices and investments; how easily firms can enter or leave the industry; consumer behaviour, because consumers react to the prices firms set; and regulation, because governments must decide how to regulate the market (06:07). *(Slide 3; Slide 4; 06:07)*

**Key points:**

- Market structure = how competitive a market is.
- It influences firms' pricing and investment decisions.
- It influences entry and exit into the industry.
- It also affects consumers and government regulation (transcript only).

## 2. The four questions used to classify a market

To decide which market structure we are looking at, the lecture uses four questions (Slide 5):
1. How many firms are there?
2. How easy is it for firms to enter or leave the market?
3. Are products identical or differentiated?
4. How responsive is demand to price?

The lecturer said that normally these four questions are enough to identify the structure: are there many firms, a few large players, or only one seller; are there high barriers to entry or free entry; are the products identical or differentiated; and how does demand react if a firm changes its price (06:07). *(Slide 5; 06:07)*

**Key points:**

- Four classifying questions: number of firms, ease of entry/exit, product type, responsiveness of demand.
- "Differentiated" means similar but not identical (54:03).
- "Barriers to entry" are things that make it difficult or impossible for new firms to join the market.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> Question 4 (responsiveness of demand) is essentially about price elasticity of demand for the individual firm: the more identical the products and the more rivals there are, the more demand for one firm's product reacts to a price change. The slides do not use the word "elasticity" here, except on Slide 12 ("perfectly elastic").

## 3. The four market structures and the comparison table

The lecture studies four structures (Slide 6): perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition and oligopoly. The lecturer described perfect competition (very many small firms) and monopoly (one single seller) as the two **extreme** cases, with monopolistic competition and oligopoly in between (06:07).

**Key points:**

- Four structures: perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition, oligopoly.
- Perfect competition and monopoly are the two extremes.
- Perfect competition: many firms, identical products, easy entry, very little market power (Slide 7).
- Monopoly: one seller, restricted entry, unique product (transcript).
- Firm's demand curve: horizontal only under perfect competition, downward sloping in the other three.

**Examples:**

- Monopoly examples mentioned in the recording: many prescription drugs, a local water company, an energy company, a train operator on a particular route (11:50).

## 4. Market share and concentration ratios

The lecturer introduced **market share** (this definition is in the recording, not in the slide text): a firm's market share = that firm's sales ÷ total market sales, where total market sales is the sum of the sales of all firms in the market. Generally, when the number of firms falls, each firm's control over the market rises (11:50).

Slide 8 defines the **concentration ratio**: the combined market share of the largest firms in an industry. For example, the five-firm concentration ratio is the combined market share of the five largest firms. A higher concentration ratio means a more concentrated market and generally less competition. Concentration can be affected by economies of scale and barriers to entry (Slide 8; 11:50). *(Slide 8; 11:50)*

**Key points:**

- Market share = firm's sales ÷ total market sales (transcript).
- Concentration ratio = sum of the market shares of the largest N firms.
- Higher concentration ratio → more concentrated market → generally less competition.
- Economies of scale and barriers to entry push concentration up.

**Examples:**

- The five-firm concentration ratio is the most commonly used example (Slide 8; 11:50).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The concentration ratio is only an indicator, not a proof of weak competition: a market can be concentrated and still be competitive (for example if entry is easy or if imports are strong). The slides say only that it "gives an indication" of the degree of competition.

## 6. Perfect competition: assumptions

Slide 12 lists the characteristics (assumptions) of perfect competition: a large number of buyers and sellers; firms are small relative to the market; identical (homogeneous) products; no barriers to entry or exit; perfect information; and firms are **price takers**. The slide adds that the individual firm's demand is **perfectly elastic at the market price** — that is the horizontal demand line.

"Price taker" means the firm is too small to influence the market price, so it simply accepts the price that the market sets. The lecturer illustrated this: if the market price is £5, the firm can sell 10 units or 1,000 units and the price per unit is still £5 (17:55). *(Slide 12; Slide 18; 17:55; 35:49)*

**Key points:**

- Many small buyers and sellers; each firm is tiny relative to the market.
- Products are identical, so buyers do not care which firm they buy from.
- Free entry and exit; perfect information.
- Firms are price takers; firm demand is perfectly elastic (horizontal) at the market price.

**Examples:**

- Agriculture: many farmers selling very similar products; wheat from one farmer is essentially the same as wheat from another, so no single farmer can set the price (Slide 18; 17:55).
- The lecturer also used carrots as an example: "carrot here, carrot there, the same thing" (35:49).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> Perfect competition is a theoretical benchmark rather than a description of real markets. The slides and the lecturer only say that agriculture "approximates" it (Slide 18).

## 7. Perfect competition: the market diagram and the firm diagram

Slide 13 shows two graphs side by side. Panel (a) is the whole industry: market demand (D) and market supply (S) cross at the equilibrium, which determines the equilibrium price Pe (and equilibrium quantity). Note the different horizontal axes — millions of units for the market, thousands for the firm.

Panel (b) is one individual firm. Because the firm is a price taker, its demand curve is a **horizontal line at Pe**. On that line, price = average revenue = marginal revenue: **Pe = AR = MR**. The lecturer explained why: average revenue is revenue per unit sold, i.e. the price per unit; marginal revenue is the extra revenue from selling one more unit. Since every extra unit sells at the same £5, AR and MR are both equal to £5 and both equal the price (17:55). *(Slide 13; 17:55)*

**Key points:**

- The market (industry) determines the price; the individual firm accepts it.
- The firm's demand curve is horizontal: D = AR = MR = Pe.
- AR = total revenue per unit = price; MR = extra revenue from one more unit.
- The two panels use different scales (millions vs thousands).

**Examples:**

- If the market price is £5, the firm receives £5 per unit whether it sells 10 or 1,000 units (17:55).

## 8. Perfect competition: short-run profit maximisation (two stages)

Slide 14 gives the two-stage method that is then reused for every market structure in this lecture:

**Stage 1 – find the profit-maximising output:** choose the output where **MR = MC** (marginal revenue equals marginal cost). On the graph this is where the MC curve crosses the horizontal MR line; the slide labels this output Qe.

**Stage 2 – measure profit:** at that output, compare average revenue and average cost. Profit = (AR − AC) × Qe. The lecturer explained the logic: AR − AC is revenue per unit minus cost per unit, which is **profit per unit**; multiplying by the quantity sold turns it into **total profit**. On the diagram this total profit is the shaded rectangle (24:04).

Under perfect competition, at the optimum MR = MC = Pe (Slide 14). *(Slide 14; 24:04)*

**Key points:**

- Stage 1: output where MR = MC.
- Stage 2: profit = (AR − AC) × Q.
- AR − AC = profit per unit; × Q = total profit = shaded rectangle.
- In perfect competition the optimum also satisfies MR = MC = price.

**Examples:**

- On Slide 14 the firm's AC lies below AR at Qe, so the firm makes a positive (supernormal) profit.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The reason MR = MC maximises profit: while the extra revenue from a unit is bigger than its extra cost, producing it adds to profit; once extra cost exceeds extra revenue, it reduces profit. The lecture states the rule but (in these sources) does not spell out this justification.

## 9. Perfect competition: short-run loss minimisation

Not every firm makes a profit. Slide 15 shows the case where, at the output chosen by MR = MC, **AC is above AR** (cost per unit is higher than price per unit). Then (AR − AC) is negative, so (AR − AC) × Q is a loss.

The important point on the slide is that **the same MR = MC rule applies whether the firm earns a profit or makes a loss** — in the loss case it gives the output where the loss is as small as possible (loss minimisation). The lecturer stressed the contrast: before, the average cost curve lay below the price; now it lies above the price (24:04). *(Slide 15; 24:04)*

**Key points:**

- MR = MC is used in both cases: profit maximisation or loss minimisation.
- If AC > AR at that output, the firm makes a loss.
- Loss size is still measured as (AR − AC) × Q, but the result is negative.
- The loss is minimised at MC = MR.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The textbook distinction between a short-run loss (where a firm may still continue if it covers its variable costs) and shutting down is not covered in these slides, so do not assume it was taught here.

## 10. Perfect competition: entry and the long run

Slide 16 shows the key long-run chain for perfect competition:

**Supernormal profit → new firms enter → market supply increases → price falls → profit falls.**

Start from a situation where price P1 is above AC, so existing firms make **supernormal profit**. Because there are no barriers to entry, new firms want this profit and join the market. In the industry diagram, market supply shifts to the right, which pushes the market price down (from P1 to PL). For the individual firm, the horizontal demand/AR line therefore falls. Entry continues until firms earn only **normal profit** — defined on the slide as economic profit of zero: the firm earns just enough revenue to cover all its costs, including the owner's opportunity cost (Slide 16).

The lecturer described the same process step by step and noted that at the new price the firm's demand line just touches the average cost curve at one point, so price per unit = cost per unit and the supernormal profit has disappeared. At that point entering the market is no longer attractive (29:56). *(Slide 16; 29:56)*

**Key points:**

- Supernormal profit = price (AR) above average cost.
- Normal profit = zero economic profit; all costs, including opportunity cost, are covered.
- Free entry removes supernormal profit over time.
- Mechanism: entry → supply shifts right → market price falls → firm's AR line falls.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> Symmetrically, if firms were making losses, some would exit, supply would shift left and the price would rise back towards normal profit. The slides only show the entry direction, so treat exit as background knowledge.

## 11. Perfect competition: long-run equilibrium

Slide 17 gives the long-run result. In long-run equilibrium the firm earns normal profit: total revenue = total cost, so economic profit = 0. The firm still produces where MR = MC, and it is also producing at the **minimum of average cost** — the lowest possible average cost.

The condition written on the slide is: **P = AR = MR = MC = AC**, with LRAC = (SR)AC = (SR)MC = MR = AR at that single point. The lecturer repeated this chain of equalities and pointed out on the diagram that the short-run average cost curve (blue) and the long-run average cost curve (green) are both at their minimum at this output (29:56). *(Slide 17; 29:56)*

**Key points:**

- Long run: normal profit, TR = TC, economic profit = 0.
- P = AR = MR = MC = AC at the equilibrium output.
- Production happens at the minimum point of average cost.
- LRAC = SRAC = SRMC = MR = AR at that point.

## 12. Perfect competition: advantages and disadvantages

Slide 18 lists the pros and cons. Advantages: (1) production at minimum cost; (2) competition encourages efficiency, because strong competition puts pressure on firms to be efficient; (3) little need for advertising, since there is little reason to spend a lot on advertising when products are identical.

Disadvantages: (1) less product variety; (2) lower profits may reduce investment; (3) less competition through product design and differentiation. In the recording the lecturer explained efficiency as producing "without waste, usually without waste in time and money" (35:49). *(Slide 18; 35:49)*

**Key points:**

- Pros: minimum-cost production, pressure to be efficient, little wasteful advertising.
- Cons: little variety, low profits may limit investment, no competition through design/differentiation.
- Efficiency here means producing without waste (transcript wording).

**Examples:**

- Agriculture again: many sellers, similar products, individual farmers cannot set the market price (Slide 18).

## 13. Monopoly: definition and barriers to entry

Slide 20 defines **monopoly** as a market structure with a single seller. The **fundamental cause of monopoly is barriers to entry**, and these barriers protect the monopolist from competition. The slide divides them into two types:

- **Structural / natural barriers** — characteristics of the industry itself, for example when starting the business requires an extremely large investment.
- **Strategic / artificial barriers** — things the firm does, for example an exclusive contract: the firm signs agreements that stop rivals accessing key distributors.

The lecturer used the example of developing an electricity network, which needs a huge investment, as a natural barrier, and explained the exclusive contract as an agreement that prevents distributors from working with any other firm (35:49). *(Slide 20; 35:49)*

**Key points:**

- Monopoly = single seller in the market.
- Barriers to entry are the fundamental cause of monopoly.
- Natural/structural barriers come from the nature of the industry (e.g. huge investment needed).
- Strategic/artificial barriers are created by the firm (e.g. exclusive contracts).

**Examples:**

- Building an electricity network/grid — extremely expensive, so hard to enter (Slide 21; 35:49).
- Exclusive contracts blocking rivals' access to distributors (Slide 20; 35:49).

## 14. Monopoly: three sources of barriers

Slide 21 groups the sources of barriers into three:

1. **Economies of scale / natural monopoly** — one large firm may produce at a lower average cost than several smaller firms, so it is difficult for a small entrant to compete on cost.
2. **Control of key resources** — a firm may control an essential input, which makes entry difficult for rivals. The historical example given is De Beers and the supply of diamonds.
3. **Exclusive contracts / legal protection** — patents and intellectual property rights can prevent competitors from producing the same product.

The lecturer linked point 2 back to earlier material: resources are the inputs used to produce outputs, so if one company controls them, others cannot easily enter (35:49). Note that Slide 2 says the "natural monopoly" part of the textbook (page 202) is *not* in the required reading. *(Slide 21; Slide 2; 35:49)*

**Key points:**

- Economies of scale can make one big firm cheaper than many small ones (natural monopoly).
- Controlling an essential input blocks rivals.
- Patents / IP rights and exclusive contracts give legal protection from competition.

**Examples:**

- De Beers and diamond supply (control of a key resource) (Slide 21; 35:49).
- Electricity grid (economies of scale) (35:49).

## 15. Monopoly: why MR lies below AR

Because a monopolist is the only seller, the demand curve it faces is the **whole market demand curve**, and it is **downward sloping**: to sell more output the firm must lower its price. On the diagram, P = AR = D (Slide 22).

Marginal revenue then lies **below** average revenue. The reason given on the slide: lowering the price to sell one more unit also reduces the revenue received on all the units that could previously have been sold at the higher price. The lecturer repeated that only perfect competition has a horizontal demand curve; monopoly (and the other structures) have downward-sloping demand, with MR below AR (35:49). *(Slide 22; 35:49)*

**Key points:**

- A monopolist faces the market demand curve: downward sloping.
- To sell more, it must cut the price.
- MR < AR, because the price cut also applies to units that would have sold at the higher price.
- Vertical axis = cost and revenue (£); horizontal axis = output (Q).

## 16. Monopoly: profit-maximising output and total profit

Slides 23–24 apply exactly the same two-stage method as under perfect competition. The slide states clearly: "a monopolist follows the same profit-maximisation rule as any other firm".

**Stage 1:** set MR = MC; this determines the output Qm (Slide 23).
**Stage 2:** at Qm, profit per unit = AR − AC, so total profit = (AR − AC) × Qm, shown as a shaded rectangle representing total economic profit (Slide 24).

The lecturer worked through both stages on the graph: find where MR crosses MC, read off Qm, read AR (on the demand curve) and AC above that output, take the difference and multiply by the quantity (35:49; 42:03). *(Slide 23; Slide 24; 35:49; 42:03)*

**Key points:**

- Same rule as all firms: MR = MC gives the profit-maximising output Qm.
- Profit per unit = AR − AC; total profit = (AR − AC) × Qm.
- The shaded rectangle on the diagram = total economic profit.
- The price charged is read from the demand (AR) curve at Qm, not from the MR curve.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> A common exam mistake is to read the price off the MR curve. Because MR lies below AR, the monopoly price is always higher than its marginal revenue at Qm. The slides imply this but do not warn about it.

## 17. Monopoly: potential entry, cost advantage and deterring entry with a lower price

Slides 25–26 show how a monopolist can keep rivals out. Very large supernormal profits attract new rivals who want to break into the industry. There are three firms in the example: **Firm M**, the incumbent monopolist, and **Firms A and B**, potential entrants. The price P1 is the revenue per unit, and each firm's AC curve shows its cost per unit.

At price P1: Firm A has AC_A > P1, so it would make a loss and does not enter. Firm B has AC_B < P1, so it would make a profit and may enter. Firm M has the lowest AC curve of all (AC_M < P1), which is why it earns large supernormal profits — a **cost advantage** (Slide 25).

To stop Firm B entering, Firm M **reduces its price from P1 to PL**, chosen so that PL = AC_B. At that price Firm B would earn only normal profit, which is much less attractive, while Firm M (with lower costs) still makes a good profit. The conclusion on the slide: a lower price can make entry less profitable for potential rivals (Slide 26; 42:03). *(Slide 25; Slide 26; 42:03)*

**Key points:**

- Compare price per unit (P) with each firm's cost per unit (AC) to see who can profitably enter.
- AC above price → loss → do not enter; AC below price → profit → may enter.
- The monopolist's advantage here is having the lowest AC curve.
- Cutting the price to PL = AC_B leaves the rival only normal profit, so entry is deterred, while the incumbent still profits.

**Examples:**

- Firm A: AC_A > P1 → loss → stays out. Firm B: AC_B < P1 → profit → may enter (Slide 25).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> This behaviour is usually called "limit pricing" or "entry-deterring pricing" in textbooks; the slides do not use that name, so learn the mechanism rather than the label.

## 18. Monopoly: advantages and disadvantages

Slide 27 lists both sides. Disadvantages: (1) higher prices; (2) lower output; (3) less competitive pressure, so the firm may become inefficient; (4) possible **X-inefficiency**, defined on the slide as not producing at the lowest possible cost; (5) potentially weaker incentives to innovate.

Advantages: (1) economies of scale; (2) large profits may finance investment; (3) competition for corporate control — the threat of takeover can pressure managers to improve efficiency. The lecturer explained economies of scale as: the more output produced, the lower the average cost per unit, and repeated that the takeover threat puts pressure on managers (48:02). *(Slide 27; 48:02)*

**Key points:**

- Cons: higher price, lower output, weak competitive pressure, X-inefficiency, weaker innovation incentives.
- X-inefficiency = not producing at the lowest possible cost.
- Pros: economies of scale, profits available to finance investment, discipline from the threat of takeover.

## 19. Monopoly vs perfect competition: price and output

Slides 28–29 put the two extremes on one diagram. Under monopoly, output is chosen where MR = MC, giving output Q1 and price P1 read from the demand curve (Slide 28).

Under perfect competition, output is where the market demand curve crosses the market supply curve, and the supply curve is represented by MC: **D = MC**. This gives a **lower price P2 and a higher output Q2** (Slide 29).

The conclusion on the slide is: **monopoly → higher price, lower output**. This is also the answer to the Mentimeter question on Slide 30: compared with perfect competition, a profit-maximising monopoly normally produces less output at a higher price (Slide 30; 48:02). *(Slide 28; Slide 29; Slide 30; 48:02)*

**Key points:**

- Monopoly: MR = MC → output Q1, price P1.
- Perfect competition: D = MC → output Q2 > Q1, price P2 < P1.
- Key result: monopoly means higher price and lower output.
- The difference arises because MR lies below AR for the monopolist.

**Examples:**

- Quiz answer (Slide 30): "Less output at a higher price" (48:02).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The underlying reason is on Slide 22: because MR < AR for a monopolist, setting MR = MC stops at a smaller quantity than setting price (AR) = MC, which is what competition forces firms to do.

## 20. Monopolistic competition: characteristics

Slide 31–32 describe monopolistic competition: **many firms, but differentiated products**. The three assumptions on Slide 32 are: (1) many or several firms; (2) free entry; (3) firms sell differentiated products — similar but not identical.

The key consequence: because products are differentiated, each firm has **some control over its price** (unlike a price taker in perfect competition). The slide's example is restaurants and cafés: there are many of them, but each tries to be different through food quality, menu, location, atmosphere, branding or service. In the recording the lecturer added that universities can be another example of monopolistic competition: similar but not identical, with different quality, atmosphere and branding (54:03). *(Slide 10; Slide 31; Slide 32; 17:55; 54:03)*

**Key points:**

- Many firms + free entry + differentiated (similar but not identical) products.
- Differentiation gives each firm some price-setting power.
- Each firm therefore faces a downward-sloping demand curve.
- This was the answer to the earlier quiz on Slide 10 (many firms, differentiated products, easy entry).

**Examples:**

- Restaurants and cafés competing on food quality, menu, location, atmosphere, branding and service (Slide 32).
- Universities (lecturer's own example, 54:03).

## 21. Monopolistic competition: short run and long run

Slide 33 summarises the two time periods. **Short run:** MR = MC, and supernormal profit is possible. Because entry is relatively easy, high profits attract new firms. **Long run:** MR = MC *and* AR = AC, so the firm earns only normal profit; competition reduces the demand faced by each existing firm.

Slide 34 shows the short run on a graph: the firm has a downward-sloping demand curve (AR = D) with MR below it; it produces Qs where MR = MC; the price Ps is read from the demand curve and may lie above average cost ACs. If Ps > AC, the firm earns supernormal profit, measured as (Ps − ACs) × Qs — the shaded rectangle (Slide 34; 54:03).

Slide 35 shows the long run. Supernormal profits attract new firms; new firms give consumers more alternatives; this **reduces the demand faced by each individual firm**, so the AR/demand curve shifts **left** to AR_L = D_L and the MR curve shifts left to MR_L too. The price falls to PL. The firm still produces where MR = MC, but now at that output **AR = AC**, so only normal profit remains. The slide's summary chain is: supernormal profit → entry → demand for each existing firm falls → profit falls → normal profit (Slide 35; 59:52). *(Slide 33; Slide 34; Slide 35; 54:03; 59:52; 1:06:11)*

**Key points:**

- Short run: MR = MC; if price > AC there is supernormal profit.
- Long run: MR = MC and AR = AC → normal profit only.
- Adjustment works through a leftward shift of each firm's demand (and MR), not through a falling market price as in perfect competition.
- Chain: supernormal profit → entry → each firm's demand falls → profit falls → normal profit.

**Examples:**

- More restaurants opening means fewer customers for each existing restaurant, so its demand curve shifts left (54:03; 59:52).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> Notice the difference in the mechanism compared with perfect competition: there, entry shifted the *market supply* curve and pushed the market price down; here, entry splits demand among more differentiated sellers, shifting each firm's *own* demand curve left. Both end in normal profit.

## 22. Monopolistic competition vs perfect competition: excess capacity

Slide 36 compares the long-run outcomes. Under perfect competition the firm's demand is the horizontal line, giving price P2 and output Q2, where output is at the minimum of LRAC. Under monopolistic competition the firm's demand D_L is downward sloping, giving a **higher price P1** and a **lower output Q1**.

The third point is **excess capacity**: the firm produces below the output level Q2 that would give the lowest average cost. The lecturer asked students to remember three things: monopolistic competition has (1) higher price, (2) lower output and (3) excess capacity (59:52). *(Slide 36; 59:52)*

**Key points:**

- Monopolistic competition in the long run: higher price than perfect competition.
- Lower output than perfect competition.
- Excess capacity = producing below the output that minimises average cost.
- Both still end with normal profit in the long run.

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> The cost of differentiation is that each firm operates at a scale that is too small to reach minimum average cost; the benefit (shown on Slide 18 as a disadvantage of perfect competition) is more variety for consumers. The slides do not state this trade-off explicitly in one place.

## 23. Monopolistic competition: non-price competition

Slide 37 makes the point that firms do not compete only through price. Four forms of non-price competition are listed: **product development** (better features, design or innovation), **branding and advertising** (make the product stand out), **customer service** (create a better customer experience) and **quality** (improve the product or service).

The lecturer linked this back to differentiation: because the products are similar but not identical, firms have an incentive to compete on features, design, innovation in the production process, quality, branding, advertising and overall customer experience, rather than on price alone (1:06:11). *(Slide 37; 1:06:11)*

**Key points:**

- Non-price competition follows directly from product differentiation.
- Four channels: product development, branding/advertising, customer service, quality.
- Contrast with perfect competition, where identical products mean little reason to advertise (Slide 18).

**Examples:**

- A café competing through atmosphere, branding and service rather than price (Slide 32; 54:03).

## 24. Oligopoly: key characteristics

Slide 39 defines **oligopoly**: a few firms between them share a large proportion of the industry, and the products offered are similar or identical. The key features are:

- **High barriers to entry.**
- **Firms are interdependent** — because there are only a few firms, each firm's decisions affect its competitors.
- **Firms may compete or collude.**

The slide shows an image related to OPEC (Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries). The lecturer repeated the three key features and emphasised interdependence (1:06:11). *(Slide 38; Slide 39; 1:06:11)*

**Key points:**

- Few firms with a large combined share of the market.
- Products similar or identical.
- High barriers to entry.
- Interdependence: each firm must consider rivals' reactions.
- Firms can compete or collude.

## 25. Non-collusive oligopoly: Cournot, Bertrand and the kinked demand curve

Slide 40 defines **non-collusive oligopoly**: firms do **not** make an agreement with each other — formal, informal or tacit. The key issue is that each firm must think about how its rivals may react to its decisions. Three well-known models are listed: Cournot (compete by choosing quantity), Bertrand (compete by choosing price), and the kinked demand curve (firms consider how rivals may respond to price changes).

**Cournot (Slide 41)** is a model of **duopoly** (a market with two firms). Assumptions: firms choose a quantity without knowing exactly how much the other firm will produce; production is relatively inflexible / has long lead times, so it is difficult to change quickly; the market price adjusts to the total output produced by both firms; firms sell homogeneous products. Memory hook from the slide: *Cournot = competition in quantity*.

**Bertrand (Slide 42)** is also a duopoly model, but with **price** as the choice variable. Assumptions: firms choose prices without knowing exactly what price the rival will choose; prices are set in advance and cannot easily be changed; but firms can adjust output quickly — no capacity constraints; products are homogeneous. Memory hook: *Bertrand = competition in price*.

The lecturer suggested comparing them to remember them: in Cournot the quantity is hard to change but the price adjusts quickly; in Bertrand the price is hard to change but output can adjust quickly; in both, products are homogeneous (1:12:05).

**Kinked demand curve (Slide 43)** focuses on how competitors react to price changes. Assumptions: if a firm cuts its price, rivals are expected to follow; if a firm raises its price, rivals are expected **not** to follow — because by not following they may attract some of that firm's customers. This creates a **kink** in the firm's demand curve, and the model helps explain why prices in oligopoly may be relatively stable (Slide 43; 1:12:05). *(Slide 40; Slide 41; Slide 42; Slide 43; Slide 44; 1:12:05; 1:18:05)*

**Key points:**

- Non-collusive = no agreement of any kind (formal, informal or tacit).
- Cournot: firms choose quantity; output inflexible, price adjusts.
- Bertrand: firms choose price; price fixed in advance, output flexible.
- Both Cournot and Bertrand assume duopoly and homogeneous products, with decisions made without knowing the rival's choice.
- Kinked demand curve: rivals follow price cuts but not price rises → kink → relatively stable prices.

**Examples:**

- Quiz answer (Slide 44): "Cournot — quantity; Bertrand — price" (1:18:05).

> [!note] Interpretation (not stated directly in the sources)
> A simple way to see the kink: if you raise your price and nobody follows, you lose many customers (demand is elastic above the kink); if you cut your price and everybody follows, you gain few extra customers (demand is less elastic below the kink). This elasticity explanation is standard background, not stated in these slides.

## How the ideas connect

- The four classifying questions on Slide 5 are exactly the columns of the comparison table on Slide 7, and they are then used to introduce each of the four structures in turn.
- The number of firms links to market share and concentration ratios: fewer firms → higher market share per firm → higher concentration ratio → generally less competition (Slide 8; 11:50).
- The same two-stage method (Stage 1: MR = MC; Stage 2: profit = (AR − AC) × Q) is reused for perfect competition (Slide 14), monopoly (Slides 23–24) and monopolistic competition (Slide 34), so learning it once covers three structures.
- Barriers to entry are the hinge between the structures: no barriers (perfect competition, monopolistic competition) means supernormal profit disappears in the long run; high barriers (monopoly, oligopoly) means supernormal profit can last.
- The shape of the firm's demand curve explains the price/output results: a horizontal demand curve (perfect competition) gives P = MR, while a downward-sloping curve (monopoly, monopolistic competition) gives MR < AR, so MR = MC stops at a lower output and higher price.
- Product differentiation (Slide 32) both gives firms some price-setting power and creates the incentive for non-price competition (Slide 37); it also explains excess capacity in the long run (Slide 36).
- Long-run adjustment works through different channels in the two 'free entry' structures: a rightward shift of market supply and a falling market price in perfect competition (Slide 16) versus a leftward shift of each firm's own demand curve in monopolistic competition (Slide 35).
- Monopoly entry deterrence (Slides 25–26) and oligopoly interdependence (Slide 39) share the same idea: firms make decisions while thinking about how actual or potential rivals will react.

## Recap

1. Market structure describes how competitive a market is, and it matters because it affects how firms behave — their prices, output, investment and entry decisions (Slide 4).
2. Classify a market with four questions: how many firms, how easy is entry/exit, are products identical or differentiated, and how responsive is demand to price (Slide 5).
3. Concentration ratios (e.g. the 5-firm ratio) measure the combined market share of the largest firms: higher ratio → more concentrated → generally less competition, as in UK motor vehicles (≈64%) versus food products (≈11%) (Slides 8–9).
4. In perfect competition firms are price takers facing a horizontal demand curve (P = AR = MR); they maximise profit (or minimise loss) where MR = MC, and profit = (AR − AC) × Q (Slides 12–15).
5. With free entry, supernormal profit attracts new firms, supply rises, price falls, and in long-run equilibrium P = AR = MR = MC = AC at minimum average cost, i.e. normal profit (Slides 16–17).
6. A monopoly exists because of barriers to entry (economies of scale, control of key resources, legal protection/exclusive contracts); it faces a downward-sloping demand curve with MR below AR, so compared with perfect competition it produces less output at a higher price (Slides 20–22, 29).
7. Monopolistic competition has many firms, free entry and differentiated products: supernormal profit in the short run, but entry shifts each firm's demand left until AR = AC, leaving normal profit, a higher price, lower output and excess capacity compared with perfect competition — and competition also takes non-price forms (Slides 32–37).
8. Oligopoly means a few interdependent firms behind high barriers, who may compete or collude; the main non-collusive models are Cournot (choose quantity), Bertrand (choose price) and the kinked demand curve (rivals follow price cuts but not price rises, so prices are relatively stable) (Slides 39–43).
